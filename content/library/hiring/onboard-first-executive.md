Onboard your first executive with a written 90-day plan you both sign off on before day one. Name the measures, the first visible win, and the decisions that move from you to them. Meet often in the first weeks, review progress weekly, and introduce them to every peer and key customer yourself. Then step back from the work you handed over. Studies collected by McKinsey find that **27%** to **46%** of executive transitions end up rated failures or disappointments two years in. The same McKinsey review finds that **68%** of transitions founder on politics, culture, and people. Not legal, tax, or compensation advice.

:::takeaways
- Write the plan before the start date. Outcomes for day 30, 60, and 90, one early win, and the decisions you hand over.
- Spend founder time early. Ben Horowitz tells CEOs to plan a huge amount of time integrating a new executive and to set monthly, weekly, and even daily objectives.
- Introduce them to every peer yourself. In an Egon Zehnder survey of more than **500** executives, **69%** cited a poor grasp of how the organization works as a cause of onboarding failure and **26%** cited lack of skill.
- Give it about six months. Elad Gil expects three months to come up to speed and three more before a senior hire is valuable. In McKinsey’s data, **62%** of external hires said real impact took at least six months.
- Measure against the plan. There is no reliable public benchmark yet for time to productivity or first-year executive attrition at Seed to Series B companies.
:::

This page covers the first VP or C-level hire at a Seed to Series B company, usually the first person who runs a function the founder ran. To pick which seat comes first, read the [Series A leadership hiring sequence](/library/hiring/series-a-leadership-hiring-sequence/). For the sales seat, read [when to hire the first VP of Sales](/library/hiring/when-to-hire-first-vp-sales/).

## Why the first 90 days decide it

:::highlight
New executives tend to stumble on how the company works and on peers they have not won over.
:::

Michael Watkins, in *The First 90 Days*, defines the breakeven point as the moment a new leader has added as much value as they consumed. He asked **210** CEOs and presidents how long a typical midlevel manager takes to reach it. The average answer was **6.2** months. A first executive at a startup carries more context debt than a midlevel manager at a large company, because much of the context lives in the founder’s head.

| Finding | Number | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Executive transitions judged failures or disappointments after two years | **27%** to **46%** | McKinsey, Keller and Meaney, 2018 |
| Transitions that founder on politics, culture, and people | **68%** | McKinsey, 2018 |
| External executive hires who find mentors or buddy networks helpful | **47%** | McKinsey, 2018 |
| External executive hires who rate standard orientation effective | **19%** | McKinsey, 2018 |
| Direct reports more likely to disengage or leave when their leader struggles | **20%** | McKinsey, 2018 |
| External hires who took far more than 90 days to reach full productivity | **92%** | McKinsey, 2018 |
| Organizations that use tailored coaching or customized assimilation plans, which McKinsey says can double the odds of success | **32%** | McKinsey, 2018 |
| Executive hires terminated, leaving, or below expectations after six to twelve months | **25%** to **40%** | Watkins, IMD, 2017 |

The McKinsey and Watkins figures come mostly from large companies. There is no reliable public benchmark yet for first-executive failure at Seed to Series B. Read them as a warning about direction. They do not predict your odds.

Orientation and a buddy leave the hard part untouched. A new VP needs to learn who decides what, which promises the founder already made, and which peers can stall their work. Only the founder can teach most of that at an early company.

## Write the plan before day one

:::highlight
If the plan lives only in your head, you will grade the new executive against a target they have not seen.
:::

Draft the plan during the offer stage and finish it with the hire in their first week. Keep it to one page. Elad Gil recommends 30-, 60-, and 90-day goals for new hires. For an executive, add the decisions that change hands and the first visible win.

| Plan element | What to write | Example for a first VP Engineering |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Outcomes by day 30, 60, 90 | Two or three results per checkpoint, each one you can check | Day 30: on-call rotation owned. Day 60: hiring plan approved. Day 90: release cadence set |
| Measures | The numbers you will review and where they come from | Deploy frequency, open incidents, offers accepted |
| First visible win | One result the whole company will notice inside the first 90 days | Fix the bug customers raise most often |
| Decisions that move to them | Named decisions they now make without you | Engineering hires below director, tool spend under a set limit |
| Decisions you keep for now | Named decisions that still need you, and when that changes | Architecture changes that affect pricing |
| People to meet | Every peer, each direct report, top customers, key board members | List with owner and target week |
| Review rhythm | Weekly progress review, plus a written check-in at day 30, 60, and 90 | Fixed slot on both calendars |

The VEN session view from a founder who has built several executive teams: write a 90-day plan with measures you both agree on, meet often in the early weeks, and review progress every week. Agree an early win so the rest of the company sees the hire working. Judgment from a [VEN session](https://www.venexecs.com/?utm_source=founderdecisions&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=library&utm_content=onboard-first-executive), not a survey.

## Run the first 90 days

:::highlight
Spend founder time early, then pull back on a schedule both of you can see.
:::

Ben Horowitz’s rule for bringing a big-company executive into a startup is aggressive integration. Set objectives they must produce against from the start. Make them learn the product, the technology, the customers, and the market. Consider a daily meeting where they bring the day’s open questions, and answer from first principles. Give them a list of people to meet and ask for a written report on what they learned from each one.

:::steps First 90 days
1. **Before day one.** Share the draft plan, the board deck, and the last two quarters of metrics. Book the peer and customer meetings.
2. **Week one.** Agree the plan together. Announce the role, what they own, and who reports to them to the whole company.
3. **Weeks one to four.** Meet often. Take their questions in each meeting and answer them in depth (Horowitz).
4. **Weeks two to six.** They meet every peer, each direct report, and the top customers, then send you a short note on what they heard.
5. **Day 30 check-in.** Review the plan in writing. Confirm the first win is on track and fix any gap in authority now.
6. **Day 60 check-in.** Move the next set of decisions to them. Cut your meetings with them back to the weekly review.
7. **Day 90 review.** Score each outcome against the measures. Rewrite the role description together for the next six months.
:::

If you can run only one item from Horowitz’s list, run the questions meeting. A new executive who brings no questions has stopped learning or is hiding what they do not know. Horowitz goes further: if they are not coming up to speed in **30** days, fire them. Most founders will want more evidence than that. Write the day 30 check-in so it gives you that evidence.

## Hand over real authority

:::highlight
Decide which decisions move to the new executive, write them down, and stop making them yourself.
:::

The most common failure after the start date is a founder who keeps the decisions. The new VP becomes a senior individual contributor with a VP title. Elad Gil’s version: the previous owner of an area often lingers to get credit for past work. Acknowledge that work, then ramp them down fast so the new person can take ownership.

| You hand over | You keep for now | Signal you held on too long |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hiring inside their function, within the approved plan | Executive hires and comp outside the plan | Their team still asks you for approval |
| Day-to-day priorities and process for the function | Changes that touch pricing, the roadmap promise, or the board plan | Peers route around them to reach you |
| Vendor and tool choices under a spending limit | Spending above the limit | You rewrite their decisions in meetings |
| The function’s weekly metrics and the story behind them | What goes in the board deck | You present their numbers at the board |

The VEN session view on authority: the authority and resources you hand over should match the responsibility in the plan. Decide what you stop carrying before the hire starts, and check that the company is ready for the role as well as checking the candidate. Revisit the role description with the new leader within the first 100 days. If the role is wrong, fix it using the plan and the evidence rather than a feeling. Judgment from a [VEN session](https://www.venexecs.com/?utm_source=founderdecisions&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=library&utm_content=onboard-first-executive), not a survey.

## Integrate them with peers

:::highlight
You hired them for skill. Relationships and culture are where they will stumble.
:::

In 2013, Egon Zehnder surveyed more than **500** experienced executives on why new hires struggled during onboarding. Watkins published the breakdown with the 2017 HBR article “Onboarding Isn’t Enough.”

| Cause of onboarding failure | Respondents citing it |
| --- | --- |
| Poor grasp of how the organization works | **69%** |
| Misfit with operational culture | **65%** |
| Difficulty forging alliances with peers | **57%** |
| Lack of understanding of the business model | **48%** |
| Ineffective decision making | **31%** |
| Disagreement over strategy | **28%** |
| Lack of experience or skill | **26%** |

The top three causes describe the company and its people. A follow-up survey by Genesis Advisers and Egon Zehnder in 2016 found new leaders got the least support from their peers. At a startup the peers are often early employees who reported to the founder before the new executive arrived. Introduce the hire yourself. Tell the early team what the new executive owns and what changes for them. Ask each peer to name one thing they need from the new function in the first 90 days.

## How to tell it is working

:::highlight
Judge the hire against the plan you wrote. A gut check at month four measures your mood.
:::

Elad Gil sets the clock for any senior hire: about three months to come up to speed on the company, product, people, and processes, and another three months before they start to be valuable. He also expects quick wins in the first weeks or months that take some pressure off the CEO. McKinsey’s data points the same way: **92%** of external hires took far more than 90 days to reach full productivity, and **62%** said real impact took at least six months. Treat day 90 as a checkpoint with a written review. It is too early for a final verdict.

| Checkpoint | Working | Worry |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Day 30 | Brings sharp questions. Has met every peer. First win scoped | No questions. Waits for meetings to come to them |
| Day 60 | Owns the decisions you handed over. Peers go to them first | Team still escalates to you. Plan unchanged and untested |
| Day 90 | First win delivered. Measures moving or the gap explained | Outcomes missed with no account of why |
| Month six | Running the function without your weekly input | You still run the weekly priorities for the function |

Horowitz names the mismatch to watch for in a big-company hire: the executive who waits for email, calls, and meetings to arrive. At a startup nothing happens unless they start it. If the day 30 and day 60 check-ins show that pattern, raise it in the weekly review with the plan in front of you both.

There is no reliable public benchmark yet for how long a first executive at a Seed to Series B company takes to reach full productivity, or how many leave inside the first year. Track your own: start date, the day each plan outcome was met, and whether the hire is still in the seat at month 12 and month 18.

## Mistakes that sink the first executive

:::highlight
Each one leaves a capable hire guessing what the job is.
:::

**No written plan.** The hire guesses what success means, and you grade them on a version they have not seen.

**Disappearing after week one.** You hired an executive to get time back, and you pull your time out before they have the context. Spend it early.

**Keeping the decisions.** A VP who needs your sign-off on each hire in their own team has a title without the job.

**Skipping the peer work.** The top three causes in the Egon Zehnder survey are about how the company works, its culture, and peers.

**Treating orientation as onboarding.** Only **19%** of external executive hires in McKinsey’s data rate standard orientation effective. Laptop, benefits, and a welcome lunch cover the easy part.

**Hiring the wrong phase and blaming onboarding.** If the role needs a builder and you hired someone who scales a finished function, a better plan will not save it. Name the phase in the offer, as the [Series A sequence page](/library/hiring/series-a-leadership-hiring-sequence/) sets out.
