A letter of intent (LOI) is the buyer's written offer: price, structure, timeline, and an exclusivity period during which you stop talking to other buyers. The price terms are usually non-binding. Exclusivity and confidentiality are binding. That split is the whole game. Once you sign, the buyer gets weeks of private diligence and you lose your other bidders, so the LOI price is the most you should expect, not a starting point. Lawyers at Orrick and Baker McKenzie put exclusivity at **30–60** days. Axial's review of **75** LOIs that broke in 2025 found technology deals had sat under exclusivity for **94** days on average before they died. Settle the terms that move cash before you sign: how the price turns into equity value, the working capital target, escrow, the earnout, what happens to employee equity, and when exclusivity ends. Not legal, tax, or investment advice.

:::takeaways
- Exclusivity is the binding part. Keep it short, with a hard end date and automatic termination if the buyer cuts the price.
- Broken deals run long. Axial's broken 2025 LOIs had sat under exclusivity **94** days in technology and **125** days with corporate buyers.
- "Non-binding" still has teeth. A Delaware court awarded **$113M** in damages over a bad-faith breach tied to a non-binding term sheet (SIGA v. PharmAthene).
- Write the price mechanics down. **93%** of 2025 private deals had a post-closing price adjustment, and working capital was a metric in **88%** of those (SRS Acquiom).
- Whatever the LOI leaves vague tends to get settled in the buyer's favor in the purchase agreement.
:::

This page covers the letter itself, clause by clause. For the whole sale, from buyer list to close, read [how to sell a startup](/library/fundraising/how-to-sell-a-startup/). If part of the offer is paid later, read [earnout terms](/library/fundraising/earnout/). To get ahead of the buyer's accountants, read [quality of earnings report](/library/fundraising/quality-of-earnings/).

## What is binding and what is not

:::highlight
The price is a promise to keep talking. The no-shop is a contract.
:::

Baker McKenzie's US private M&A guide describes the usual split. Price and deal terms are not binding. Confidentiality, standstill, and exclusivity are. Break fees are rare in private deals and, if they appear at all, go into the purchase agreement rather than the LOI. The guide also warns that a careless letter can create binding commitments by accident, and that an LOI can support a claim if one side fails to negotiate in good faith.

| Clause | Usually binding? | What it does | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Price and structure | No | Sets the number and the form (cash, stock, earnout) | Baker McKenzie |
| Exclusivity or no-shop | Yes | You stop soliciting or talking to other buyers, often **30–60** days | Baker McKenzie; Orrick 2024 |
| Confidentiality | Yes | Usually points back to the NDA you already signed | Baker McKenzie |
| Break fee | Rare | Common in public deals, rare in private ones | Baker McKenzie |
| Good-faith negotiation | Depends on wording | Can create liability if one side walks in bad faith | Baker McKenzie; SIGA v. PharmAthene |

The case to know is SIGA Technologies v. PharmAthene. The parties signed a license term sheet marked non-binding, then a merger fell apart. The Delaware courts found SIGA had broken a contractual duty to negotiate in good faith toward terms consistent with that term sheet. In 2015 the Delaware Supreme Court upheld an award of **$113,116,985** in expectation damages. With interest and fees, SIGA's SEC filing put the judgment at **$194.6M**. Read the "non-binding" paragraph as carefully as the price.

## What the LOI should settle

:::highlight
If a term moves cash to you, put it in the letter, even in one line.
:::

Buyers resist moving off the LOI later, which works for you only on terms the LOI states. A vague term becomes the buyer's draft of the purchase agreement. These are the clauses that decide your proceeds:

| Term | Ask the LOI to say | Why it matters | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Price basis | Enterprise value, then the bridge to equity value: cash added, debt and transaction expenses subtracted | Debt-like items such as deferred revenue or accrued bonuses can come off the price | SRS Acquiom 2026 |
| Working capital | The target and how it is measured | Working capital was an adjustment metric in **88%** of 2025 deals with a price adjustment | SRS Acquiom 2026 |
| Escrow | Size, length, and whether it is the only remedy | Median general escrow **10%** without deal insurance, **0.5%** with it | SRS Acquiom 2026 |
| Price-adjustment escrow | A separate, capped amount | In **72%** of 2025 deals, median **0.97%** of deal value | SRS Acquiom 2026 |
| Earnout | Metric, period, and acceleration, or none | Earnouts in **24%** of 2025 non-life-sciences deals | SRS Acquiom 2026 |
| Employee equity | Assumed, cashed out, or cancelled; whether unvested options count in the price | Assumed unvested options inside the headline inflate what stockholders get | Editorial |
| Retention pool | Funded by the buyer on top of the price, or carved out of it | A carve-out moves money from all sellers to the people who stay | Editorial |
| Founder terms | Re-vesting, rollover, non-compete length | Proceeds that depend on staying are pay, not price | Editorial |
| Closing conditions | A short, named list | Each open condition is a later chance to reprice | Baker McKenzie |

The working capital line looks technical and is not. In SRS Acquiom's 2026 study of more than **1,570** deals with a finalized adjustment, buyers made an initial claim in **57%**. The median final claim was **0.27%** of deal value, and **19.6%** of claims reached **1%** or more. The method, not the number, usually decides who wins. Agree the measurement method in the LOI and do a [quality of earnings](/library/fundraising/quality-of-earnings/) review before buyers see your numbers.

## Exclusivity: short, dated, with exits

:::highlight
Time under exclusivity works for the buyer. Each week, it learns more and you have fewer options.
:::

Orrick's M&A partners put typical exclusivity at **30–60** days and target **45** days from LOI to close, with about **60** typical. That is the plan. Deals that fail drift long. Axial publishes data on its members' LOIs that broke:

| Buyer type, broken 2025 LOIs | Average days under exclusivity before breaking | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Family office | **37** | Axial Dead Deal Report |
| Search fund | **70** | Axial Dead Deal Report |
| Private equity fund | **106** | Axial Dead Deal Report |
| Corporation | **125** | Axial Dead Deal Report |
| Independent sponsor | **129** | Axial Dead Deal Report |
| Technology deals, any buyer | **94** | Axial Dead Deal Report |

Axial's sample is lower middle market companies priced on EBITDA, so read it as a direction rather than a venture benchmark. There is no reliable public benchmark yet for exclusivity length or post-LOI price changes on venture-backed software sales.

Write the exits into the clause. Exclusivity should end on a fixed date, end at once if the buyer lowers the price or adds a financing condition, and extend only against milestones you can see: the buyer's first draft of the purchase agreement by a date, confirmatory diligence done by a date. Keep your runway plan and one other buyer warm so an extension is your choice.

## Why the LOI price is a ceiling

:::highlight
Prices rarely rise after the LOI. Bring every fact that helps the price to the table before you sign.
:::

Axial's 2025 data shows how deals break after signing. Diligence findings outside the accounting review ended **25.3%** of broken LOIs. Earnings that did not hold up in the buyer's quality of earnings review ended **21.3%**. Failed renegotiation ended **14.7%**. Each of those starts as a request to cut the price.

Founders who have sold say the same in VEN sessions. Treat the LOI number as the most you will get, grade your red flags and clear them before you sign, and use an inbound offer to start a competitive process rather than letting the first buyer set the timetable. Nothing said to the buyer's team during diligence is off the record, including at dinner ([VEN session](https://www.venexecs.com/?utm_source=founderdecisions&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=library&utm_content=letter-of-intent)). 1984 Ventures gives the same advice from the investor side: when an LOI arrives, get a second term sheet, and keep the first buyer's name confidential.

## Example: two LOIs at the same headline

Illustrative arithmetic, not data from any deal. Two buyers each offer **$50M**.

| Line | LOI A, vague | LOI B, specific |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Headline | $50.0M | $50.0M |
| Retention pool | Not mentioned; buyer later carves **$3.0M** out of the price | Buyer funds it on top |
| Escrow held at close | "Customary"; lands at **10%**, **$5.0M** for 12 months | **0.5%** with deal insurance, **$0.25M**, plus a capped adjustment escrow of about **1%**, **$0.5M** |
| Cash to sellers at close, before fees and preferences | **$42.0M** | **$49.25M** |
| Working capital claim, paid from escrow later | "To be agreed"; the buyer's method produces a **1%** claim, **$0.5M** | Method fixed in the LOI; a median-size claim, **0.27%**, about **$0.14M** |

Same headline, **$7.25M** apart at close in this example. Some of LOI A's escrow may come back after 12 months, but you plan around cash at close, and deal insurance has a premium the LOI should assign. Run both through the [cap table waterfall calculator](/tools/cap-table-waterfall/) to see what reaches common stock after the preference stack.

:::steps Negotiate an LOI
1. **Get a second bid first.** Your bargaining power ends when you sign.
2. **Ask for equity value, not only enterprise value.** List what counts as debt and transaction expenses.
3. **Fix the working capital method.** Name the target and the accounting rules.
4. **Size the escrow.** Price deal insurance against a **10%** cash holdback.
5. **Name the employee terms.** Who funds the retention pool, what happens to unvested options, and what founders must re-vest.
6. **Decide on the earnout now.** If there is one, set the metric and the period in the LOI.
7. **Shorten exclusivity.** Thirty to 45 days, a hard end date, and termination on any price cut.
8. **Check the binding paragraph.** Have counsel confirm which clauses bind and what the good-faith language commits you to.
9. **Clear your red flags.** Do your own diligence before the buyer does.
:::

## Decision table by situation

:::highlight
Sign when the LOI states the terms that move your cash, and the clock is short enough that you can still walk.
:::

| Situation | What to do | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One inbound buyer, no process | Use the offer to contact two or three others before signing | A deadline set to stop you shopping |
| Two LOIs at similar prices | Compare cash at close and terms, not headlines | Retention pools carved from the price |
| Buyer wants **90** days of exclusivity | Counter with **30–45** and milestone-based extensions | Automatic renewal language |
| Buyer asks to cut the price in diligence | Ask for the finding in writing; your exclusivity should end on a cut | A new price with the same clock |
| Strategic buyer paying partly in its stock | Name the share price method, lock-up, and registration in the LOI | Stock valued at a date that helps the buyer |
| Runway under six months | Shorter exclusivity matters more than a higher headline | A buyer who knows your cash date |
