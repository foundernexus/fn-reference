To sell a startup, build relationships with likely buyers a year or two before you want an offer, then run a short process with at least two bidders. Price and terms only move while a second buyer is live. Once you sign a letter of intent (LOI), you stop talking to anyone else, so negotiate exclusivity down toward **30** days; Orrick's M&A partners say it usually runs **30–60**. Expect a large share of the price to arrive after closing. In SRS Acquiom's 2026 study of more than **2,300** private-target deals, **88%** of 2025 deals held back part of the price in an escrow or holdback, and the median escrow on deals without deal insurance was **10%** of the price. Not legal, tax, or investment advice.

:::takeaways
- Start early. 1984 Ventures' handbook says build buyer relationships a year or two out; with **3–6** months of cash left, you get a fire sale.
- Get a second bid before you sign. Orrick: LOI exclusivity usually runs **30–60** days; target **45** days from LOI to close, expect about **60**.
- Read the money, not the headline. SRS Acquiom 2025: median escrow **10%** without deal insurance, **0.5%** with it; claims window median **12** months.
- Price an earnout at what earnouts pay. SRS Acquiom: earnouts appeared in **24%** of 2025 deals outside life sciences and have paid about **21** cents on the dollar.
- After a priced round, the board and usually a majority of preferred holders must approve the sale (1984 Ventures).
:::

This page covers the sale process from first buyer conversation to closing. For the liquidation preference waterfall that decides who gets paid, start with [term sheet red flags](/library/fundraising/term-sheet-red-flags/). If one customer carries the revenue, read [customer concentration risk](/library/finance/customer-concentration/) first, because buyers move that risk into the earnout. If cash is the reason you are selling, use [when runway runs out](/library/fundraising/when-runway-runs-out/) to weigh a sale against a bridge or a wind-down.

## Know who buys startups and what they pay

:::highlight
About six in ten startup acquisitions on Carta happen at Seed to Series B, and a closed deal does not mean the common stock got paid.
:::

Carta counted **502** acquisitions among US startups on its platform in the first half of 2025, about **1.2%** of them. The rate by stage:

| Stage on Carta | Acquired in H1 2025 | Companies at that stage | Rate | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pre-seed (no priced round) | 138 | 26,114 | **0.5%** | Carta, Walker 2025 |
| Seed | 113 | 6,485 | **1.7%** | Carta, Walker 2025 |
| Series A | 150 | 6,006 | **2.5%** | Carta, Walker 2025 |
| Series B | 54 | 2,673 | **2.0%** | Carta, Walker 2025 |
| Series C | 29 | 1,142 | **2.5%** | Carta, Walker 2025 |

Excluding pre-seed, the rate is **2.1%**. Peter Walker's caveat on the same data: Carta cannot see the outcome of each deal, and many early acquisitions are likely acquihires. The market has kept moving: Carta reports **421** M&A exits in the first half of 2026, up **16%** year over year, and investors quoted in the same piece say a strong AI story now helps a company line up an exit.

SRS Acquiom's 2025 deals give the typical shape of a venture-backed sale. The median company sold **6.7** years after its first investment, after **3** equity rounds and **$26M** of equity raised. The median deal returned **3.3×** the equity invested. There is no reliable public benchmark yet for sale price multiples at Seed to Series B; the SRS figures cover deals of all sizes. When the price does not clear the preferences, some deals set aside a slice for management; SRS found a management carveout in **4.3%** of 2025 deals.

## Build the buyer list before you need it

:::highlight
List the companies that would gain most from owning you, and know a senior executive at each one before any number comes up.
:::

1984 Ventures' handbook sorts buyers into three groups, each with a different reason to pay:

| Buyer group | Why they buy | Who to know |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Larger competitors | Your customers, team, or product closes a gap | CEO, general manager, or product owner |
| Partners and customers of your product | They already depend on it; owning it lowers cost or risk | The executive who owns the integration |
| Large companies threatened by your competitors | Buying you is a defensive move | The executive losing deals to that competitor |

The handbook puts the third group as the least obvious and sometimes the best outcome. It advises spending time with a CEO, GM, or other senior executive rather than corporate development, which executes deals but rarely starts them. Check what each buyer can afford: the same handbook says companies typically spend at most **1–2%** of their market value on one acquisition, so a competitor worth **$8B** tops out around **$100–150M**.

## Decide on a banker and know the fees

:::highlight
At Seed to Series B, a founder-led process with strong counsel is common. Hire a banker when you need reach beyond your own network or a full auction.
:::

1984 Ventures says a banker adds most at Series C or later with more than **$50M** of ARR, when you are running a contested process, or when the deal needs heavy modeling. Earlier, or with one interested buyer you already know, a banker can signal that you are shopping the company. Either way, hire transaction counsel who closes M&A deals often.

Firmex's 2024–25 survey of **212** US middle-market advisors, mostly sell-side, gives the usual fee shape:

| Deal size | Typical success fee | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $5M | **5.7%** | Firmex US M&A Fee Guide 2024–25 |
| $20M | **4.0%** | Firmex US M&A Fee Guide 2024–25 |
| $100M | **2.4%** | Firmex US M&A Fee Guide 2024–25 |
| $150M | **2.1%** | Firmex US M&A Fee Guide 2024–25 |
| Engagement fee | One-time **$26,000–50,000**, credited against the success fee | Firmex US M&A Fee Guide 2024–25 |

The most common monthly work fee was **$5,000–10,000**. Firmex notes the success fee is payable at closing, so ask how your banker's fee treats an earnout or escrow that may never arrive.

## Get a second bid, then sign a short LOI

:::highlight
Treat the LOI price as the most you will get, and use the weeks before you sign it to bring in a second buyer.
:::

Most LOI terms are non-binding, but the letter sets price, structure, and an exclusivity period during which you cannot talk to other buyers. 1984 Ventures' advice on receiving one: get a second term sheet right away, and keep the first buyer's name confidential. Orrick's timeline, from its M&A partners Mark Seneca and Justin Yi:

| Step | Typical length | What happens | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sign the LOI | Day 0 | Exclusivity starts, usually **30–60** days | Orrick 2024 |
| LOI to signed purchase agreement | **30–60** days | Diligence, management meetings, drafting | Orrick 2024 |
| Signing to closing | A week for many private deals; months with antitrust review | Consents, shareholder approval, waiting periods | Orrick 2024 |
| Whole path, LOI to close | Target **45** days; typically **60** | Longer with regulatory approval | Orrick 2024 |

In SRS Acquiom's 2025 data, **43%** of deals signed and closed on the same day and **57%** signed first and closed later. A US deal above **$133.9M** may need a Hart-Scott-Rodino filing with the FTC and Justice Department and a waiting period before close; the filing fee is **$35,000** below **$189.6M** (FTC, 2026 thresholds in the Federal Register). Ask counsel early if your price is near that line.

Read each LOI term that moves cash: the payment at close, the escrow size and length, any earnout metric, the treatment of unvested employee equity, and retention packages for you and the team.

## Price the escrow, the earnout, and the claims window

:::highlight
Headline price minus escrow, minus a discounted earnout, minus fees and the preference stack, is what you plan around.
:::

SRS Acquiom's 2026 Deal Terms Study on 2025 deals:

| Term | 2025 figure | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deals with an escrow or holdback | **88%** | SRS Acquiom 2026 |
| Median general escrow, no deal insurance identified | **10.0%** of transaction value | SRS Acquiom 2026 |
| Median general escrow, deal insurance identified | **0.5%** of transaction value | SRS Acquiom 2026 |
| Deals with representations and warranties insurance identified | About **46%** | SRS Acquiom 2026 |
| Median survival period for general claims | **12** months | SRS Acquiom 2026 |
| Deals with an earnout (outside life sciences) | **24%** | SRS Acquiom 2026 |
| Median earnout potential as a share of the closing payment | **34%** | SRS Acquiom 2026 |
| Payout across deals with earnouts | About **21** cents per dollar | SRS Acquiom, 2024 Claims Insights |

Illustrative arithmetic, not data from any deal. A buyer offers **$40M** at closing plus an earnout of up to **$13.6M** (the **34%** median), with no deal insurance:

| Line | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Paid at closing | $40.0M |
| Escrow at **10%**, held about **12** months | −$4.0M |
| Cash on the closing day | $36.0M |
| Earnout potential | $13.6M |
| Earnout at about **21** cents per dollar | About $2.9M |

The headline reads **$53.6M**. Planning cash is **$36M** on day one, most of the **$4M** escrow a year later, and perhaps **$2.9M** of earnout. Fees come off the top, and preferred holders take their preferences before common. Run your own waterfall with counsel.

When a buyer buys deal insurance, the median escrow falls from **10%** to **0.5%**, so ask whether the buyer will buy a policy before you agree to a **10%** escrow.

## Keep the company running through diligence

:::highlight
Tell as few people as possible, keep hitting the plan, and fix small legal gaps before the buyer finds them.
:::

1984 Ventures expects **4–8** weeks of intense work between the signed LOI and the final documents, from a missing contractor agreement to an open-source component the buyer wants removed. Its advice on people: early on, only a co-founder should know; later, the CFO and a handful of key people. Employees ask two questions when they hear: how much will I make, and is my job secure. Plan those answers before the announcement.

The VEN session judgment from founders who have sold: the business must keep hitting its numbers during diligence, because a missed month after the LOI becomes a reason to cut the price. Keep the [Series A diligence checklist](/library/fundraising/series-a-diligence-checklist/) room current, and the buyer's requests arrive faster.

Not legal or tax advice. Approvals, escrow terms, tax treatment of the sale, and payments to executives depend on your charter and the deal; transaction counsel and a tax adviser should review the documents before you sign.

:::steps Run the sale
1. **List the buyers.** Competitors, partners and customers, and companies threatened by your competitors.
2. **Know one senior executive at each.** A year or two before you want an offer.
3. **Check what each can pay.** About **1–2%** of the buyer's market value is a common ceiling (1984 Ventures).
4. **Pick counsel, then decide on a banker.** Bankers fit contested or larger deals; ask how the fee treats escrow and earnouts.
5. **Line up board and preferred approval.** Read the charter and voting agreement for who must consent.
6. **Get a second bid before the LOI.** Price and terms move only while two buyers are live.
7. **Cut exclusivity toward 30 days.** Orrick puts the usual range at **30–60**.
8. **Model cash, not headline.** Escrow, earnout at about **21** cents per dollar, fees, and the preference waterfall.
9. **Ask about deal insurance.** Median escrow falls from **10%** to **0.5%** when the buyer buys a policy (SRS Acquiom).
10. **Keep the plan on track.** A miss during diligence becomes a price cut.
:::

## Mistakes that cost the deal

**Waiting until cash is short.** 1984 Ventures: with **3–6** months left, the result is a fire sale.

**Signing the first LOI alone.** Exclusivity removes your other options; get the second bid first.

**Long exclusivity.** Each extra week gives the buyer more time to find reasons to lower the price. Orrick advises sellers to push toward **30** days.

**Counting the earnout at face value.** Earnouts have paid about **21** cents per dollar (SRS Acquiom).

**Pitching corporate development first.** They run the paperwork; a business owner decides to buy (1984 Ventures).

**Telling the whole company early.** News spreads and work slows. Keep the group small until you sign.
