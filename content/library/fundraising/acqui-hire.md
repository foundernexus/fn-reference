An acqui-hire is a sale where the buyer wants your team, and maybe your code, but not your business. The buyer's money arrives in two parts. A purchase price goes to the company and runs through your liquidation preference waterfall, so preferred holders are paid first. Retention pay goes straight to the people the buyer hires, as sign-on cash and new stock that vests over **2–4** years (Foley & Lardner's Gurpreet Bal). If the price sits below the preference stack, common holders get nothing unless investors fund a management carve-out, commonly **5% to 10%** of net proceeds (Westaway). An acqui-hire beats a wind-down when the price plus a carve-out returns more to investors than liquidating would, and the team wants the jobs. Not legal or tax advice.

:::takeaways
- Two pools. The price goes to stockholders by preference; retention pay goes to hired staff and sits outside the waterfall (Westaway).
- Most acqui-hires are asset purchases. The buyer picks the IP and the people and leaves the entity, its contracts, and its debts behind (Gurpreet Bal, Foley & Lardner).
- Common gets paid through a carve-out. Westaway puts the usual size at **5% to 10%** of net proceeds; Cooley's Alex Kassai told Threshold that about half fall in **6–10%** and most of the rest in **11–15%**.
- Investors approve the deal. Selling substantially all assets takes a vote of a majority of the outstanding stock under Delaware law, and preferred holders usually hold separate approval rights.
- Write the board record. Directors owe their duty to the common, so document why the deal beats a wind-down for them (Westaway).
:::

This page covers the structure of a talent sale and who receives what. For running a full sale process with several bidders, read [how to sell a startup](/library/fundraising/how-to-sell-a-startup/). If you have not yet decided between cutting, bridging, and closing, start with [when runway runs out](/library/fundraising/when-runway-runs-out/). To see your own preference stack at a given price, use the [cap table waterfall calculator](/tools/cap-table-waterfall/). There is no reliable public benchmark yet for how acqui-hire money splits between price and retention, or for carve-out sizes by deal size.

## Acqui-hire, asset sale, or wind-down

:::highlight
All three end the company as you run it. They differ in who decides and who gets paid.
:::

Cooley says acqui-hires can be stock sales, asset sales, or, less often, mergers. Gurpreet Bal, a Foley & Lardner partner, says most are asset purchases. The table compares the three exits founders weigh most often.

| | Acqui-hire (asset purchase plus offers) | Asset sale with no hires | Wind-down (dissolution) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What the buyer takes | Selected IP, some contracts, the people it wants | IP, code, domains, customer lists | Nothing; assets are sold off or abandoned |
| Who gets paid | Price to the company, then the waterfall; retention to hired staff | Price to the company, then the waterfall | Creditors first; stockholders get what is left |
| Stockholder vote | Majority of outstanding stock for substantially all assets (DGCL § 271), plus preferred approvals | Same | Board resolution, then a majority of outstanding stock (DGCL § 275) |
| Timing to stockholders | After closing and paying debts | After closing and paying debts | No sooner than **150** days after the last claim rejection notice under the § 280 process (DGCL § 281) |
| People not hired | Severance; WARN notice if covered | Severance; WARN notice if covered | Severance; WARN notice if covered |

The WARN Act covers employers with **100** or more employees and requires **60** days' written notice before a plant closing or mass layoff (29 U.S.C. §§ 2101–2102). VLP Law Group's guide flags severance for staff who are not hired, and WARN claims, as costs to the deal.

## Where the buyer's money goes

:::highlight
The buyer sets the split between price and retention. Investors share only in the price.
:::

Westaway says retention packages come out of the buyer's budget, not the purchase price, so investors have no claim on them. Bal says retention value often exceeds the formal purchase price. That split is the negotiation. Investors and staff who are not joining want a higher price. The people the buyer hires gain from more retention.

Two large public deals show the split. Both were licenses plus hires, a structure big AI buyers use. Neither is a typical startup acqui-hire, and the figures come from press reports citing unnamed sources.

| Deal (reported) | Total | To the company and its investors | To hired staff | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Google and Windsurf, 2025 | **$2.4B** | **$1.2B** to investors; company kept over **$100M** | **$1.2B** for about **40** hires | TechCrunch |
| Microsoft and Inflection, March 2024 | about **$650M** | **$620M** license fee plus **$30M** waiver; early investors about **1.5×**, later round about **1.1×** | Not reported | TechCrunch, citing The Information |

At Windsurf, about **200** of roughly **250** employees were not hired by Google. Some hires had their stock grants revoked and their vesting restarted on a **4**-year clock, according to TechCrunch. Cognition then bought the rest of the company, reportedly for about **$250 million**, and said every employee would share in that sale.

Regulators watch the large ones. WilmerHale reported in January 2026 that the FTC chairman said the agency is looking at whether buyers structure acqui-hires to avoid Hart-Scott-Rodino merger filings.

## What preferred and common holders receive

:::highlight
Below the preference stack, common gets nothing unless investors give some of their recovery away.
:::

Start with the equity value: the price after debt, deal expenses, and any working capital shortfall. Add up every series' liquidation preference. If the equity value is below that total, preferred takes all of it (Westaway). Option holders usually get nothing, and the merger agreement cancels their options at closing.

| Who | Purchase price below the stack | Purchase price above the stack | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Preferred holders | All of it, by seniority, less any carve-out | Preference or convert, whichever pays more | Westaway |
| Common and vested options | Zero, unless a carve-out pays them | What is left after preferences | Westaway |
| SAFE holders | Depends on whether the deal triggers the SAFE; often a fraction of the money in | Converts or cashes out per the SAFE | Gurpreet Bal |
| Hired staff | Retention pay from the buyer, outside the waterfall | Retention pay plus their share as common | Westaway; Gurpreet Bal |
| Staff not hired | Severance, and their common if any | Severance plus their common | VLP Law Group |

Carve-outs are the usual fix. A management carve-out plan sets aside a share of net proceeds for a named group of employees, paid ahead of the preferred. Investors fund it from their own recovery, so the preferred have to approve it in writing before signing.

| Carve-out size | Figure | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Common range | **5% to 10%** of net proceeds, higher in very small deals | Westaway, October 2026 |
| What Cooley saw in 2023 | about half at **6–10%**, most of the rest at **11–15%** | Heidi Roizen, Threshold Ventures, citing Alex Kassai of Cooley |
| One worked case | **12.5%** of net sale price | Threshold Ventures |
| Time to agree and approve | at least **30–60** days | Threshold Ventures |

Carve-out and retention payments are pay, not sale proceeds. Expect payroll withholding, and check the golden parachute rules under Section 280G and the deferred compensation rules if any payment lands after closing (Westaway). Most founders paid little for their shares, so the mix of cash, stock, and pay changes what they keep (Cooley).

**Labeled example (editorial arithmetic).** You raised **$8M** of 1× non-participating preferred. A buyer offers **$9M** in total: **$3M** of price and **$6M** of retention for five engineers. Preferred takes the **$3M** and common gets zero. A **10%** carve-out pays **$300,000** to the team and leaves preferred **$2.7M**. If instead you wind down and **$1M** is left after creditors and severance, preferred gets **$1M**, months later. The acqui-hire pays the preferred more and gives the team jobs. Your investors will still push to move money from retention into price: at **$5M** of price and **$4M** of retention, preferred gets **$4.5M** after the same carve-out.

## Retention packages and the board record

:::highlight
Retention pay ties the team to the buyer. The board record protects the directors who approve it.
:::

Bal says retention most often means sign-on bonuses or restricted stock units vesting over **2–4** years, sometimes with earnout payments tied to staying. Westaway describes cash or new equity paid over **1–3** years. Cooley says buyers often ask key hires to re-vest some of their own shares, so part of their sale money depends on staying.

Watch the line between price and pay. If the price is low and retention runs far above market, investors can argue that you relabeled price as pay to go around the preference (Westaway). Keep retention terms at market and tied to real work after closing.

Directors owe their duty to the common, even when the preferred control the board. Westaway says Delaware courts have reviewed sales where the preferred took everything under entire fairness, the strictest standard. Delaware's 2025 amendments to Section 144 add a safe harbor for conflicted deals approved by a majority of disinterested directors, or a committee of at least two, with the material facts disclosed. Founders who get large retention offers are conflicted too.

Clean IP decides price. Bal says gaps in invention assignment, such as contractors who never signed or a cofounder who left without papers, can cut the price or kill the deal. Check yours against [IP assignment](/library/equity/ip-assignment/) before you talk to a buyer.

:::steps Run an acqui-hire without losing the cap table
1. **Model three outcomes.** The offer, a wind-down after creditors and severance, and a second buyer if one exists. Run each through the waterfall.
2. **Ask the buyer for the split in writing.** Price, retention pool, who is on the list, and the vesting. Put whether retention is inside or on top of the price in the LOI. Show your lead investor the offer before you negotiate your own package ([VEN session](https://www.venexecs.com/?utm_source=founderdecisions&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=library&utm_content=acqui-hire)).
3. **Read your SAFE and note terms.** Find out whether an asset sale or a license triggers them, and what holders get ([SAFE vs priced round](/library/fundraising/safe-vs-priced-round/)).
4. **Propose a carve-out early.** Size it, name the participants, and get board and preferred approval in writing before signing.
5. **Separate the conflicted people.** Founders and investor directors disclose their interest; disinterested directors lead if you have them.
6. **Plan for staff who are not hired.** Severance, final payroll, benefits, and WARN notice if you are covered.
7. **Clean up IP assignments.** Every founder, employee, and contractor who touched the code.
8. **Close the shell.** Pay or reserve for claims, then distribute and dissolve.
:::

## Decision table by situation

:::highlight
Take a talent deal when it returns more than a wind-down and the team wants the jobs.
:::

| Situation | Do this | Skip this |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cash runs out before a raise can close, and one buyer wants the team | Model the offer against a wind-down; propose a carve-out | Waiting for a second buyer you cannot name |
| Price below the preference stack | Carve-out for common, approved by preferred before signing | Telling staff their options will pay |
| Buyer offers rich retention and a token price | Ask investors for their minimum price; move money into price until they approve | Signing before preferred approval |
| Buyer wants five of thirty people | Severance and WARN check for the rest; fund it in the price | Leaving the shell with unpaid payroll |
| Two buyers interested | Run a short process on total value, price, and retention | Taking the first talent offer |
| Company still growing and fundable | Raise or bridge, and say no | Selling the team at a seed price |
