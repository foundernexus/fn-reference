A strategic buyer usually pays more for a venture-backed startup that is growing and not yet profitable. Across disclosed software deals in 2026 through September, strategic buyers paid a median **6.1x** trailing revenue and private equity paid **4.0x** (Kroll, Fall 2026). A financial buyer can pay more once you have real EBITDA. PitchBook's Q3 2026 report puts the trailing 12-month median at **11.5x** EBITDA for sponsors and **8.9x** for strategics in North America and Europe. The price is only part of it. A strategic mostly pays cash and folds you into its product. A private equity fund buys with debt, asks you to roll part of your proceeds into the new company, and expects you to run it for years. Not legal, tax, or investment advice.

:::takeaways
- Growing and burning cash: expect the best price from a strategic. Kroll's 2026 software medians: **6.1x** revenue for strategics, **4.0x** for PE.
- Profitable at scale: PE can win. PitchBook: sponsors paid **11.5x** EBITDA over the trailing 12 months vs **8.9x** for strategics, the narrowest gap since 2023.
- PE prices off debt it can raise. GF Data: average debt on new platform buyouts fell to **2.9x** EBITDA in Q2 2026, and the average deal price fell to **7.0x**.
- Rollover is the PE signature. In GF Data's 2025 platform deals through Q3, **68.3%** included seller rollover, averaging **14.8%** of enterprise value.
- Your job changes either way. A strategic integrates you. PE keeps you as an operator with a management pool of **7.5–15%** that vests over four to five years (Dechert).
:::

This page compares the two buyer types on price, deal structure, and what you do after close. For the sale process itself, from the first buyer conversation to closing, start with [how to sell a startup](/library/fundraising/how-to-sell-a-startup/). If the buyer mostly wants the team, read [acqui-hire](/library/fundraising/acqui-hire/). Before any buyer prices you on EBITDA, fix your numbers with a [quality of earnings report](/library/fundraising/quality-of-earnings/).

## Who pays more: multiples by buyer type

:::highlight
Strategics pay more per dollar of revenue. Sponsors pay more per dollar of EBITDA. Know which number a buyer will price you on.
:::

A strategic buyer is an operating company that buys you to grow its own business. A financial buyer, usually a private equity fund, buys you as an investment it plans to sell later (SRS Acquiom definitions). The data points in two directions because each buyer type prices a different number.

| Measure | Strategic | Private equity | Deals covered | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Median EV / trailing revenue | **6.1x** | **4.0x** | Disclosed global software deals, 2026 to 30 September | Kroll, Fall 2026 |
| Median EV / EBITDA, trailing 12 months | **8.9x** | **11.5x** | North America and Europe, all sectors | PitchBook Q3 2026 |
| Median EV / EBITDA, 2026 to date | **8.9x** | **10.9x** | North America and Europe, all sectors | PitchBook Q3 2026 |
| Average deal value, 2025 | **$241.6M** (US public buyer) | **$224.4M** | US private-company sales | SRS Acquiom 2026 |
| Average deal value as a multiple of equity invested, 2025 | **8.4x** (US public buyer) | **7.2x** | US private-company sales | SRS Acquiom 2026 |

Kroll counts acquisitions by PE-backed platforms as PE deals. PitchBook says tighter financing cut sponsor multiples by a full turn over the trailing 12 months, while strategics dropped a little over half a turn. The SRS averages come from venture-backed and other private-company sales of all sizes. The median sale in that study returned **3.3x** the equity invested, so large outcomes pull the averages up.

An older academic result points the same way for companies without large profits. In 1,292 cash acquisitions of US public companies from 1990 to 2005, target shareholders got a **63%** higher premium when the buyer was a public company rather than a private equity firm (Bargeron, Schlingemann, Stulz, and Zutter, Journal of Financial Economics, 2008).

There is no reliable public benchmark yet for sale multiples by buyer type for venture-backed companies at Seed to Series B. Most of these companies have no EBITDA to price, so the revenue figures are the closer guide.

## What a private equity buyer can pay, by deal size

:::highlight
PE prices off normalized EBITDA and the debt that EBITDA can carry. Smaller deals get lower multiples.
:::

GF Data collects completed deals from more than **330** private equity firms in the **$10M–$500M** enterprise value range. Its average multiples of trailing adjusted EBITDA:

| Enterprise value | Average, 2003–Q3 2025 | Deals | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $10M–$25M | **5.9x** | 2,084 | GF Data, Q3 2025 |
| $25M–$50M | **6.7x** | 1,671 | GF Data, Q3 2025 |
| $50M–$100M | **7.7x** | 1,156 | GF Data, Q3 2025 |
| $100M–$250M | **8.6x** | 654 | GF Data, Q3 2025 |
| $250M–$500M | **9.7x** | 104 | GF Data, Q3 2025 |

The latest quarter is lower. GF Data's average fell to **7.0x** in Q2 2026 from **7.3x** in Q1, which it attributes to a mix of smaller deals. Mercer Capital's read of the same data puts Q2 2026 near **6.8x** for $10M–$50M deals and **7.6x** for $50M–$250M, with technology at **5.7x**. Buyer transaction costs add about **0.3x** on top.

Debt sets the ceiling. GF Data says average total debt on new platform buyouts fell to **2.9x** EBITDA in Q2 2026 from **3.4x** in Q1, and senior debt pricing rose to **7.8%**. Less debt per dollar of EBITDA means less a sponsor can pay for the same company. GF Data also notes that private equity prices generally leave out the strategic value a corporate buyer adds, which is why its multiples run below strategic deal prices.

## How each buyer structures the price

:::highlight
A strategic hands you cash or its stock. PE hands you cash plus a stake in the new company you have to keep growing.
:::

Across 2025 US private-company sales, **51%** paid all cash, **21%** paid cash plus management rollover, **20%** paid a mix of cash and stock, and **8%** paid all stock (SRS Acquiom). Stock means the buyer's own shares, which a fund does not have to offer.

| Term | Strategic buyer | Private equity buyer | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Payment | Cash, buyer stock, or both | Cash funded partly by debt, plus rollover | SRS Acquiom 2026; GF Data |
| Rollover equity | Rare | In **68.3%** of 2025 platform deals, averaging **14.8%** of enterprise value | GF Data, Q3 2025 |
| Debt in the deal | Usually none on your company | **2.9x** EBITDA average on Q2 2026 platform buyouts | GF Data, Q2 2026 |

Rollover is value deferred, not a discount. Your rolled stake pays out when the fund sells the company, and you cannot sell it before then. Dechert notes that sponsors often hold preferred equity and offer rollover investors some of each type, so read where your rolled shares rank before you agree on a percentage. Escrows and earnouts apply to both buyer types; the [sale process page](/library/fundraising/how-to-sell-a-startup/) has the SRS Acquiom medians. Ask tax counsel early whether the rollover can be structured to defer tax on the rolled portion, and check how it affects [QSBS](/library/equity/qsbs/).

## Your job after close

:::highlight
Sell to a strategic and you join a larger company. Sell to PE and you still run one, with a new board and a plan to sell again.
:::

**Strategic buyer.** Most strategic SaaS deals are tuck-ins that add a capability to an existing platform (Software Equity Group's 3Q26 report, as quoted by Blossom Street Ventures). Your product, team, and customers move into the buyer's org. You still negotiate the retention package and your title, and you do it before the letter of intent, not after. If the buyer wants the engineers more than the product, the deal is closer to an [acqui-hire](/library/fundraising/acqui-hire/).

**Private equity buyer.** The fund needs an operator, and that is often you. Dechert's private equity guide says management equity pools typically run **7.5–15%** of fully diluted equity, higher when the sponsor holds more preferred. Time-based awards vest over four to five years on the US East Coast and sometimes less in the West. They typically accelerate when the sponsor exits, and a termination for cause commonly forfeits vested and unvested equity. You report to a board the sponsor controls, against a plan that ends in another sale.

## The hybrid: a PE-backed company as the buyer

:::highlight
About one buyer in four is both. A PE-owned company bidding for you prices like a strategic and structures like a fund.
:::

Many acquirers sit between the two types. In SRS Acquiom's 2025 data, **32%** of buyers were US public companies, **14%** were US private equity funds, **23%** were US private companies backed by private equity, **16%** were other US private companies, and **15%** came from outside the US. In SaaS, Software Equity Group counted PE-backed strategics in **17.8%** of 3Q26 deals and direct platform investments by funds in **9.2%**.

A PE-backed platform buying you as an add-on can pay for product fit the way a strategic does. It funds the deal from the sponsor and its debt, and it may ask for rollover into the platform. Treat it as a strategic on price and a fund on terms.

## Example: one company, two bids

Editorial example, not a valuation. A software company has **$20M** of trailing revenue and little EBITDA. At Kroll's 2026 medians, a strategic at **6.1x** is a **$122M** bid and a PE buyer at **4.0x** is **$80M**. If the PE buyer asks for GF Data's average rollover of **14.8%** of enterprise value, about **$11.8M** of the $80M stays in the new company as equity instead of cash at close. Both bids then lose escrow, any earnout discount, fees, and the preference stack before common sees cash. Run each bid through the [cap table waterfall calculator](/tools/cap-table-waterfall/).

:::steps Run a sale with both buyer types
1. **Score your profile.** Revenue growth and product fit favor a strategic. Steady EBITDA and cash conversion favor PE.
2. **Build two lists.** Strategics that would gain most from owning you, and funds or PE-backed platforms in your category.
3. **Get your numbers diligence-ready.** PE prices off normalized EBITDA, so finish a sell-side quality of earnings review first.
4. **Ask each bidder what it is pricing.** Revenue, EBITDA, or a strategic number. Ask what debt the PE bid assumes.
5. **Compare cash at close.** Subtract rollover, escrow, a discounted earnout, fees, and the preference stack from each bid.
6. **Price the stock.** For a stock bid, check lockups and how the buyer's share price has moved. For rollover, check where your shares rank.
7. **Negotiate your role before the LOI.** Retention and title from a strategic. Pool size, vesting, and leaver terms from PE.
:::

## Decision table by situation

:::highlight
Match the buyer to the number you can show and the job you want next.
:::

| Situation | Lean toward | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fast growth, negative EBITDA | Strategic | A low PE bid built on EBITDA you do not have |
| Profitable with steady cash flow | Run both; PE can win | Debt assumptions in the PE bid |
| You want to stay CEO and sell again later | PE or a PE-backed platform | Leaver terms and where rollover ranks |
| You want liquidity and to step back | Strategic, all cash | A long earnout tied to your staying |
| Buyer offers its public stock | Strategic, with a collar or cash floor | Lockups and share price drops before you can sell |
| Buyer is a PE-backed company in your space | Treat as strategic on price, fund on terms | Rollover into the platform |
