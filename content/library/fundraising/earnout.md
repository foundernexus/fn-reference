An earnout is part of the sale price paid later, only if the business hits a target after close. Treat it as a discounted IOU, not price. In SRS Acquiom's 2026 study of **199** completed earnouts outside life sciences, sellers collected **21%** of the maximum earnout dollars, and only **52%** of deals paid anything. The terms decide which side of that line you land on: one metric you control, a short period, a sliding scale instead of a cliff, monthly reporting, a ban on the buyer steering around the target, and acceleration if the buyer resells the business. Not legal, tax, or investment advice.

:::takeaways
- Price it at what earnouts pay. Across **199** deals, sellers got **21%** of maximum earnout dollars; the median deal got **17%** (SRS Acquiom 2026).
- Shorter pays. **74%** of earnouts with a period of one year or less paid something, vs **49%** at one to two years (SRS Acquiom 2026).
- Revenue is the usual metric, in **69%** of 2025 earnouts. EBITDA appeared in **21%**, and it is easier for a buyer to move.
- Most protections are rare. Only **16%** of 2025 earnouts carried a commercially reasonable efforts duty and **24%** accelerated on a resale; **65%** let the buyer offset claims against the earnout.
- Fight before signing. A third of sellers contested the earnout, and only **3%** of contested deals went to a final judgment.
:::

This page covers the earnout clause line by line. For the full sale process and how the earnout fits next to escrow and fees, start with [how to sell a startup](/library/fundraising/how-to-sell-a-startup/). If one large customer is the reason the buyer wants an earnout, read [customer concentration risk](/library/finance/customer-concentration/). If you are choosing between a corporate buyer and a fund, see [strategic vs financial buyer](/library/fundraising/strategic-vs-financial-buyer/), because the payout odds differ by buyer type.

## How often earnouts pay

:::highlight
Half of earnouts pay nothing. The half that pay usually pay about half.
:::

SRS Acquiom acts as shareholder representative on private-company sales and tracks what earnouts pay out. Its 2026 Earnouts and Claims Study covers **199** non-life-sciences deals with an earnout due, **$25.5B** of transaction value, and more than **$6B** of potential earnout payments.

| Measure | Result | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deals that paid any earnout | **52%** | SRS Acquiom Earnouts and Claims 2026 |
| Share of maximum earnout dollars paid | **21%** | SRS Acquiom Earnouts and Claims 2026 |
| Average / median share paid, all deals | **32%** / **17%** | SRS Acquiom Earnouts and Claims 2026 |
| Average / median share paid, deals that paid anything | **51%** / **43%** | SRS Acquiom Earnouts and Claims 2026 |
| Same, deals up to $50M / above $50M | **55%** / **48%** average | SRS Acquiom Earnouts and Claims 2026 |

Earnouts are also common and large. SRS Acquiom's 2026 Deal Terms Study found one in **24%** of 2025 private-target deals outside life sciences, with a median potential of **34%** of the closing payment, and **60%** of those deals had more than one earnout.

## What changes the odds

:::highlight
Buyer type and length move the odds more than anything you can negotiate after close.
:::

Among deals that paid anything, SRS Acquiom splits the results by buyer, target, and period:

| Deal type | Share of deals with any payout | Average / median of maximum paid (payers only) | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Private equity buyer | **72%** | **50%** / **31%** | SRS Acquiom Earnouts and Claims 2026 |
| US strategic buyer | **38%** | **51%** / **43%** | SRS Acquiom Earnouts and Claims 2026 |
| Technology target | **66%** | **50%** / **41%** | SRS Acquiom Earnouts and Claims 2026 |
| Period of one year or less | **74%** | **58%** / **66%** | SRS Acquiom Earnouts and Claims 2026 |
| Period of one to two years | **49%** | **52%** / **40%** | SRS Acquiom Earnouts and Claims 2026 |
| Period over three years | **66%** | **42%** / **36%** | SRS Acquiom Earnouts and Claims 2026 |

Two readings for a founder. A strategic buyer that folds your product into its own is less likely to pay at all, which fits the integration risk on the [buyer comparison page](/library/fundraising/strategic-vs-financial-buyer/). And a short earnout is the best single term you can ask for. The 2025 median length was **21** months; **23%** ran a year or less and **38%** ran one to two years (SRS Acquiom Deal Terms 2026).

## Pick the metric

:::highlight
Tie the earnout to the number least exposed to the buyer's cost decisions.
:::

| Metric | Share of 2025 earnouts | What the buyer controls | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Revenue | **69%** | Pricing, bundling, sales coverage, which entity books the sale | SRS Acquiom Deal Terms 2026 |
| Earnings or EBITDA | **21%** | All of the above plus overhead allocation, headcount, and integration costs | SRS Acquiom Deal Terms 2026 |
| Other (unit sales, product launch, a milestone) | **23%** | Roadmap priority, launch timing, resourcing | SRS Acquiom Deal Terms 2026 |

Shares add to more than 100% because some earnouts use two metrics. Revenue is the founder's default for a reason. Once you sit inside a larger company, an EBITDA target absorbs every shared cost the buyer decides to allocate to you.

Define the metric down to the line item. Name the accounting principles and say they match your pre-close books, because a buyer switching revenue recognition can move the number on its own. Say how bundled deals, intercompany sales, and customers moved to another product count. If your revenue figures will be rebuilt in diligence, fix them first with a [quality of earnings](/library/fundraising/quality-of-earnings/) review so the base year is not argued later.

Ask for a sliding scale. SRS Acquiom's 2020 white paper on earnouts advises tying payments to outcomes and value points rather than a plan that will change after close. Editorial example, not deal data: a **$5M** earnout pays on reaching **$10M** of revenue. At **$9M** a cliff pays **$0**. A linear scale from **$8M** to **$10M** pays **$2.5M**.

## Covenants: what the buyer promises to do

:::highlight
Without an express promise, the buyer can run the business for its own reasons, even if that kills the earnout.
:::

| Buyer covenant | In 2025 earnouts | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reporting requirements | **93%** | SRS Acquiom Deal Terms 2026 |
| No action whose primary purpose is to harm the earnout | **82%** | SRS Acquiom Deal Terms 2026 |
| Commercially reasonable efforts to hit the earnout | **16%** | SRS Acquiom Deal Terms 2026 |
| Run the business in line with the seller's past practice | **6%** | SRS Acquiom Deal Terms 2026 |
| Run the business to maximize the earnout | **3%** | SRS Acquiom Deal Terms 2026 |
| Disclaimer of any fiduciary duty to sellers | **33%** | SRS Acquiom Deal Terms 2026 |

The common "no action intended to harm" clause is weak. The seller has to prove the buyer's purpose, not only the effect. An efforts clause is stronger when it says what effort means. In the Auris Health sale, Johnson & Johnson paid **$3.4B** upfront with up to **$2.35B** in milestones and promised efforts consistent with how it treated its own priority devices. The Delaware Court of Chancery found J&J breached that promise and awarded more than **$1B** in damages plus interest in September 2024. In January 2026 the Delaware Supreme Court reversed the part tied to the first milestone and upheld the efforts finding for the rest. The lesson: a defined benchmark ("as the buyer treats its own priority products") gave the sellers something to measure the buyer against.

Ask for reporting that does not shrink when the earnout is behind. SRS Acquiom recommends periodic written reports with the detail behind the number, access to supporting records and audit rights, and the right to meet the people running the business.

## Acceleration and offsets

:::highlight
Ask what happens to the earnout if the buyer sells you, fires you, or makes a claim.
:::

Only **24%** of 2025 earnouts accelerated, fully or partly, when the buyer sold the earnout business, usually unless the new owner took on the obligation (SRS Acquiom Deal Terms 2026). Ask for acceleration on a resale, on the buyer shutting down or folding the product, and on a breach of the covenants above.

The buyer could set off indemnity claims against future earnout payments in **65%** of 2025 earnouts. The rest were silent, and none expressly barred it. An offset right turns every post-close claim into a reason to hold back earnout money. If you cannot remove it, limit it to finally resolved claims and cap it.

## If the earnout depends on you staying

:::highlight
Tie the earnout to your employment and US GAAP makes it pay for your work, not price.
:::

Under the US accounting rule for acquisitions, ASC 805-10-55-25, an earnout that is automatically forfeited if the selling shareholder's employment ends is compensation for services after close, not purchase price (PwC Viewpoint). The buyer books it as an expense. Ask tax counsel how that changes your own tax treatment before you accept a leaver clause on earnout money. Keep earnout payments to all sellers on the same per-share formula and put retention in a separate package, the same split that matters in an [acqui-hire](/library/fundraising/acqui-hire/).

## Disputes

:::highlight
Most earnout fights settle. Few reach a judge, so the clause you sign is the case you get.
:::

In SRS Acquiom's 199-deal dataset, sellers contested the earnout in writing in **33%** of deals. Sellers got a higher payment in **35%** of contested deals, and renegotiations added **$157M** in total, **12%** of the earnout dollars paid. Only **3%** of contested deals were resolved by litigation or arbitration to a final judgment. Name an independent accountant to settle calculation disputes, with a deadline, and keep claims about buyer conduct for court or arbitration.

The failure founders describe in VEN sessions comes before any dispute. The buyer decides your product is no longer the one it wants to back, moves the team, and the earnout quietly goes to zero. The defense is in the contract: write the headcount and roadmap funding needed to hit the target into the deal, so the buyer cannot starve the earnout without paying ([VEN session](https://www.venexecs.com/?utm_source=founderdecisions&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=library&utm_content=earnout)).

## Example: what an earnout offer is worth

Illustrative arithmetic, not data from any deal. A buyer offers **$40M** at close plus up to **$13.6M** over two years, the **34%** median potential. At the **21%** dollar payout rate, plan on about **$2.9M** of earnout. The headline reads **$53.6M**. Planning value is about **$42.9M** before escrow, fees, and the preference stack. At that rate, **$1M** more at close is worth about **$4.8M** of earnout potential. Run both versions through the [cap table waterfall calculator](/tools/cap-table-waterfall/).

:::steps Negotiate an earnout
1. **Push cash first.** Every dollar moved from earnout to close is worth more than a dollar of earnout.
2. **Pick one metric you control.** Revenue over EBITDA. Define it with your pre-close accounting principles.
3. **Shorten the period.** Aim for a year or less. **74%** of those paid something, vs **49%** at one to two years.
4. **Replace the cliff with a scale.** Partial payment from a floor, so a near miss still pays.
5. **Write the buyer's effort.** A defined efforts standard tied to how it treats its own priority products, not only "no action intended to harm".
6. **Fund the target.** Put the team and roadmap budget the target needs into the agreement.
7. **Get monthly reporting and audit rights.** Same detail whether the target is on track or not.
8. **Add acceleration.** On a resale of the business, a shutdown of the product, or a covenant breach.
9. **Limit offsets.** Only finally resolved claims, with a cap.
10. **Separate your pay.** Earnout on the same per-share formula as other sellers; retention in its own agreement.
:::

## Decision table by situation

:::highlight
Accept an earnout when it bridges a real gap in price, not when it replaces cash you could get.
:::

| Situation | What to ask for | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Buyer and you disagree on next year's revenue | Short revenue earnout with a sliding scale | EBITDA swapped in late |
| One customer is a large share of revenue | Earnout tied to that customer renewing, a year or less | A long period that outlasts the contract |
| Strategic buyer will fold you into its product | Cash at close over earnout; acceleration on integration | Product sunset after close |
| PE buyer with a growth plan | Revenue earnout plus reporting and audit rights | Overhead allocated to your unit |
| Earnout forfeits if you leave | Separate retention from earnout | Compensation treatment and leaver clauses |
| Second bidder offers more cash, less earnout | Compare at the **21%** payout rate | Headline price that is mostly contingent |
