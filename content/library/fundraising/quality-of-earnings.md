A quality of earnings report (QoE) is an accountant's rebuild of your numbers from the ledger. It tests whether the revenue and earnings a buyer priced in the letter of intent (LOI) hold up. The buyer usually orders one after the LOI. You can order your own first. Do that, because the gap is now a top deal killer: in Axial's review of **75** broken 2025 LOIs, **21.3%** died on QoE EBITDA discrepancies, up from **10.6%** in 2023. For a venture-backed company the test is mostly revenue. Expect the buyer to rebuild ARR from invoices, split recurring from services revenue, and recheck gross retention and gross margin. Then expect a fight over working capital: SRS Acquiom found a post-closing price adjustment clause in **93%** of 2025 private deals. Not legal, tax, or investment advice.

:::takeaways
- QoE findings break deals. Axial 2025: QoE EBITDA discrepancies ended **21.3%** of failed LOIs, and other diligence findings ended **25.3%**.
- Buyers rebuild ARR from invoices, not your CRM. Maxwell Locke & Ritter's SaaS QoE starts there and flags CRM-based ARR as a common source of gaps.
- The price moves after close too. SRS Acquiom: **89%** of price adjustment clauses ended in a payment, and **51%** of 2025 adjustments went the buyer's way.
- A sell-side QoE is cheap next to a price cut. One provider, Midwest CPA, publishes sell-side QoE fees from **$25,000** and **2–4** weeks of work.
- Keep hitting the plan through diligence. A miss after the LOI becomes a price cut ([VEN session](https://www.venexecs.com/?utm_source=founderdecisions&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=library&utm_content=quality-of-earnings)).
:::

This page covers the buyer's financial diligence and what to fix before you sign the LOI. For buyers, bankers, exclusivity, escrow, and earnouts, read [how to sell a startup](/library/fundraising/how-to-sell-a-startup/). If one customer carries the revenue, read [customer concentration risk](/library/finance/customer-concentration/) too, because a QoE will surface it. There is no reliable public benchmark yet for QoE fees, or for how much a QoE cuts ARR or EBITDA, on venture-backed software sales.

## What a QoE is and who orders it

:::highlight
An audit asks whether past statements follow the rules. A QoE asks whether the earnings will repeat.
:::

No standard sets QoE scope. Midwest CPA calls it a consulting engagement and notes that no rule says who must perform it. Get the scope in writing.

| | Audit | Sell-side QoE | Buy-side QoE |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Who orders it | The company, for the board or lenders | You, before buyers see numbers | The buyer or its lender, after the LOI |
| What it answers | Do the statements follow GAAP? | What survives a buyer's rebuild? | What earnings are we paying for? |
| When | Each year | **6–8** weeks before you go to market or sign an LOI | During exclusivity |
| Who relies on it | Board, investors, lenders | You, then buyers as a starting point | The buyer's deal team and lenders |

The timing row is editorial. It follows Midwest CPA's **2–4** weeks of work plus time to fix what the report finds.

## Why deals break on it

:::highlight
Diligence findings now break more signed LOIs than financing does.
:::

Axial publishes why its members' signed LOIs failed. The 2025 set covers **75** broken deals.

| Why the signed LOI broke | 2023 | 2025 | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| QoE EBITDA discrepancies | **10.6%** | **21.3%** | Axial Dead Deal Report |
| Other diligence findings (legal, customers, contracts) | **19.1%** | **25.3%** | Axial Dead Deal Report |
| Financing fell through | **21.3%** | **10.7%** | Axial Dead Deal Report |
| Renegotiation failed | n/a | **14.7%** | Axial Dead Deal Report |
| Business underperformed during diligence | n/a | **8.0%** | Axial Dead Deal Report |

Read it with care. These are lower middle market deals priced on EBITDA, the sample is small, and the numbers are shares of failed deals, not a failure rate. Broken technology deals in the 2025 set sat under exclusivity for an average of **94** days before they died. One buyer in the report said the banker had overstated EBITDA by **25%**.

Bigger companies report the same problem. In Grant Thornton's Q1 2026 survey, **52%** of **185** US respondents named the quality or completeness of target financials as a barrier to closing deals.

## For a venture-backed company, the test is revenue

:::highlight
A loss-making company has little EBITDA to adjust. The buyer prices ARR, so the QoE goes after ARR.
:::

Maxwell Locke & Ritter, an Austin CPA firm, describes four pillars for a SaaS QoE. The table maps each to what usually breaks and what to fix first.

| What the buyer rebuilds | What breaks | Fix before the LOI | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ARR from invoice lines in the ledger | ARR in the deck comes from the CRM; credits, discounts, and late invoices are missing | Rebuild ARR from invoices yourself and explain every gap to the CRM number | Maxwell Locke & Ritter |
| Recurring vs non-recurring revenue | Implementation and services counted as recurring | Split them in the ledger and the deck | Maxwell Locke & Ritter |
| Gross retention from invoice data | Early or late renewals show up as fake upsells or downsells | Use one written churn definition and apply it to every month | Maxwell Locke & Ritter |
| Recurring gross margin | Support and onboarding payroll sits in the wrong cost line | Allocate people costs by job function | Maxwell Locke & Ritter |
| Deferred revenue month by month | Annual prepays recognized the wrong way; no waterfall | Build a monthly waterfall of billings, revenue released, and adjustments | PKF O'Connor Davies |
| EBITDA add-backs, if the buyer prices on earnings | One-time costs that recur, or costs a buyer must replace | Keep only add-backs you can tie to a document | Axial; Maxwell Locke & Ritter |

Usage-based revenue gets its own test. If part of your ARR is metered usage, read [usage revenue as ARR](/library/finance/usage-revenue-as-arr/) before a buyer does the math. Retention gets a second look too, so keep gross and net retention side by side as on the [NRR vs GRR](/library/finance/nrr-vs-grr-board-pack/) page.

## Where the money moves after the LOI

:::highlight
The LOI sets the headline price. Working capital and deferred revenue decide what you get at close.
:::

Most private deals true up the price after closing, based on cash, debt, and working capital delivered against a target. SRS Acquiom tracks it.

| Post-closing price adjustment | Figure | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2025 deals with a price adjustment mechanism | **93%** | SRS Acquiom 2026 Deal Terms Study |
| 2025 adjustments that use working capital | **88%** | SRS Acquiom 2026 Deal Terms Study |
| 2025 deals with a separate adjustment escrow | **72%**, median **0.97%** of deal value | SRS Acquiom 2026 Deal Terms Study |
| 2025 deals that spell out a specific calculation method | **39%** | SRS Acquiom 2026 Deal Terms Study |
| Adjustments that resulted in a payment, 2020 to Q3 2025 | **89%** | SRS Acquiom 2026 Working Capital PPA Study, **1,570+** deals |
| Final direction, 2025 (Q1–Q3) | **51%** to buyer, **38%** to seller, **11%** none | SRS Acquiom 2026 Working Capital PPA Study |
| Median final buyer claim | **0.27%** of deal value | SRS Acquiom 2026 Working Capital PPA Study |
| Buyer claims of **1%** of deal value or more | **19.6%** of claims | SRS Acquiom 2026 Working Capital PPA Study |
| Deals where sellers disputed the buyer's first calculation | about **29%** | SRS Acquiom 2026 Working Capital PPA Study |

Deferred revenue is the line that matters most for a SaaS seller. PKF O'Connor Davies sets out the fight. Buyers call it debt-like, because you kept the cash and they must deliver the service. Sellers call it working capital, an ordinary-course liability. A middle path treats only the cost to deliver as debt-like. PKF notes the seller usually carries the burden of proving the working capital case. Settle the treatment in the LOI, and back it with your gross margin.

**Labeled example (editorial arithmetic).** A **$60M** sale. An adjustment escrow at the SRS median of **0.97%** holds **$582,000**. A buyer claim at the median **0.27%** costs **$162,000**; a claim at **1%** costs **$600,000**. If the buyer wins debt-like treatment for **$3M** of deferred revenue, the equity price drops **$3M**. If only the cost to deliver it counts, and that cost is a quarter of the balance, the cut is **$750,000**.

## Sell-side or buy-side, and what it costs

:::highlight
The buyer will run a QoE either way. A sell-side report lets you find the gaps first.
:::

Most firms do not publish QoE prices. CT Acquisitions reviewed **18** QoE providers in November 2026 and found **3** that publish prices; the Big Four and national CPA firms quote per engagement. One that does publish, Midwest CPA, lists these fixed fees for smaller deals:

| Midwest CPA QoE | Published fee | Work time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deals under **$5M** | about **$15,000** | **2–4** weeks; budget about **4** |
| Deals **$5M–$15M** | **$18,000–$25,000** | **2–4** weeks |
| Deals over **$15M** | from **$25,000** | **2–4** weeks |
| Sell-side QoE | from **$25,000** | **2–4** weeks |

Treat these as one firm's floor, not the market. A venture-backed software sale that needs SaaS metrics work sits above these tiers, and those firms quote per engagement. Weigh any quote against the labeled example above: one median buyer claim on a **$60M** deal costs more than six times the published sell-side floor.

:::steps Get ready for the buyer's QoE
1. **Close the books monthly on accrual.** A QoE runs on monthly data. If you close quarterly or on cash, fix that first.
2. **Rebuild ARR from invoices.** Tie the deck number to invoice lines and explain every gap to the CRM figure.
3. **Split recurring from services revenue.** In the ledger, the deck, and the gross margin.
4. **Write down your churn and retention definitions.** Apply them to every month the buyer will see.
5. **Build the deferred revenue waterfall.** Billings in, revenue released, adjustments, by month.
6. **List every add-back with a document behind it.** Drop the ones you cannot prove.
7. **Draft your working capital target.** Use a trailing average, decide where deferred revenue sits, and bring it to the LOI.
8. **Order a sell-side QoE if a sale is likely within a year.** Finish it before you sign an LOI, so the buyer starts from your numbers.
:::

## Decision table by situation

:::highlight
The closer the LOI, the more a sell-side QoE pays.
:::

| Situation | Do this | Skip this |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Seed or Series A, no sale in view | Monthly accrual close, ARR from invoices, written churn definitions | A sell-side QoE |
| Inbound interest from a strategic buyer | ARR bridge, deferred revenue waterfall, add-back list | Signing an LOI before the working capital target is in it |
| Running a banker-led sale | Sell-side QoE finished before buyers see numbers | Starting the QoE after the first bids |
| LOI signed, buyer's QoE under way | One owner for the request list, answers within a day, keep hitting the plan | Arguing each finding; pick the ones with money behind them |
| QoE cuts ARR or EBITDA by more than the escrow | Ask for the bridge line by line and re-price only on documented items | Accepting a flat price cut without the bridge |
