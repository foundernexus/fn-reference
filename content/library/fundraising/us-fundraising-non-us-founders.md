Raise from US VCs when you are already executing on US expansion. Index Ventures says so in its guide for European founders: US investors usually expect a clear US angle, and they matter most once you target the US market. A VEN session for non-US founders set the bar for a US lead at seed or Series A: a CEO in the US, real US traction, prior US experience, or a product clearly better than the US options. A US firm joining a round someone else leads asks for less. Get in through a warm introduction. In a survey of **885** VCs, only **10%** of deals came inbound from company management (Gompers et al.). Before you sign, expect the fund to ask for a Delaware parent, or for PFIC and CFC protections if you keep your home company. US money is flowing to Europe in fewer, larger checks: US investors were in **€48.2 billion** of European rounds through September 2026, across **1,348** rounds, a deal count pacing almost **15%** below 2025 (PitchBook). Not legal or tax advice.

:::takeaways
- Match the round to your US plan. Index Ventures ties US investor involvement to the point where you are executing on US expansion.
- Lead or participant changes the bar. A US lead at seed or Series A wants a US story; a US participant follows the lead's terms (VEN session judgment).
- Warm intros win. Over **30%** of VC deals come through professional networks and **20%** from other investors' referrals; **10%** come inbound cold (Gompers et al., survey of **885** VCs).
- US checks are concentrating. PitchBook: deal value with US investors in Europe pacing **61.2%** above 2025, deal count almost **15%** below. CVCA: US investors in **25%** of Canadian deals in 2025, down from **32%**.
- Fix the structure before term sheets. Most US funds want a Delaware C corp; staying foreign means PFIC tests, QEF covenants, and no QSBS (Wilson Sonsini; Pillsbury; 26 U.S.C. § 1202).
:::

This page covers when to raise in the US, how to get meetings, what the fund will ask about your company structure, and how to run the process. If a US lead has already asked you to change your parent company, read [Delaware flip for non-US startups](/library/fundraising/delaware-flip/). If you need US hires before US money, read [US subsidiary for a foreign startup](/library/fundraising/us-subsidiary-setup/). There is no reliable public benchmark yet for the share of seed and Series A rounds in non-US startups that a US investor leads, by home region.

## Do you need US money now?

:::highlight
Take a US lead when you are building in the US. Take a US participant when the firm brings hires, customers, or partners. Skip it when the local round is full and the firm brings neither.
:::

| Your situation | Usual move | What to watch | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Seed, no US customers, no US team | Raise locally; add US angels or a transatlantic fund if you can | A US lead will price in geography risk | Index Ventures; VEN session judgment |
| Seed or Series A, CEO moving to the US or US is your first market | Pitch US leads | Plan the flip before the term sheet | Index Ventures; [Delaware flip](/library/fundraising/delaware-flip/) |
| Local lead set, US firm offers a small check | Take it if the firm helps US hiring, customers, or partners | A US name on the cap table helps US hires; a passive check adds tax covenants | Index Ventures; VEN session judgment |
| Series B, US revenue growing | Run a full process with US funds | Metrics carry more weight than location at this stage | Index Ventures (Collibra); VEN session judgment |
| Strong home market, US launch still a plan | Close at home, build metrics, return for the next round | Coming to US funds too early burns first impressions | VEN session judgment |

Index asked European founders how much a US investor matters for winning the US market: **52%** said important or extremely important, **41%** said useful or slightly useful, and **6%** called it irrelevant. Collibra's CEO, Felix van de Maele, went further in the same guide: a VC with a US network is "an absolute requirement for series B."

In November 2026, VEN ran a session with a US expansion practitioner for founders in Europe and Asia on raising from US VCs. The judgment in the table comes from that session: seed and Series A leads want a US story, participation checks are easier to win, and Series B turns on metrics. That is session judgment, not a survey.

### One accelerator's numbers on raising in San Francisco

Entrepreneurs First runs a three-month fundraising program in San Francisco. Its CEO, Alice Bentick, told Index that EF companies raising seed in the US see a **30%** valuation increase if they come from London and **50%** if from Paris, and close in **2–4** weeks. Those are EF's figures for its own portfolio, not a market benchmark. She also says founders should arrive with **20+** conversations already lined up so the round does not look stale.

## How to get the meetings

Gompers, Gornall, Kaplan and Strebulaev surveyed **885** VCs at **681** firms. Over **30%** of deals came through professional networks, almost **30%** were self-generated by the VC, **20%** were referred by other investors, **8%** by portfolio companies, and **10%** came inbound from company management. The average firm screened **200** companies to make **4** investments a year. A cold email from Porto to Silicon Valley can work, and Index tells that story about Sword Health and Khosla Ventures. Plan on the 90% instead.

Three routes produce most intros for a non-US founder:

- **Your current investors.** Ask each one for the US partners they have co-invested with. A referral from another investor is a fifth of all deals in the survey.
- **Founders of companies in the target fund's portfolio.** Portfolio referrals are a smaller share (**8%**) and a strong signal when you get one.
- **Regular time in the US.** Send investor updates to the US firms you want to know long before you raise, and ask for coffee when you are in the Bay Area or New York (VEN session judgment).

Index adds one test for any US firm you take: the partner should be willing to spend time with you in Europe, for board meetings and beyond. A transatlantic firm with partners on both sides is one answer.

## What the fund will ask about your company

US venture funds care where your parent company sits because of their own tax position and their fund documents. Pillsbury notes that a fund's governing documents often limit how much it can invest outside the US, so a request for a Delaware C corp may reflect how the managers set up the fund.

**PFIC.** A non-US corporation is a passive foreign investment company if **75%** or more of its gross income is passive, or **50%** or more of its assets produce passive income. Cash counts as a passive asset, so a startup holding round proceeds can fail the asset test (Wilson Sonsini, 31 March 2025). Gain on PFIC shares can be taxed at ordinary rates (up to **37%** federal) instead of capital gains rates (up to **20%**), plus an interest charge, unless the investor makes a qualified electing fund (QEF) election. US investors often ask for a representation that you are not a PFIC and a covenant to test it each year and provide QEF information.

**CFC.** A non-US corporation is a controlled foreign corporation when US shareholders who each own **10%** or more together own more than **50%**. Wilson Sonsini's example: a UK founder with board control moves to the US, the company becomes a CFC, the asset test switches to tax basis, and a cash-heavy startup becomes a PFIC for its smaller US investors. Talk to a US tax adviser before any founder moves.

**QSBS.** The § 1202 exclusion applies only to stock in a domestic C corporation (26 U.S.C. § 1202(d)). US investors in your foreign company get no QSBS. See [QSBS for startup founders](/library/equity/qsbs/).

**Form D.** If US investors buy under Regulation D, the company files Form D within **15** days of the first sale, counted from the date the first investor is irrevocably committed. The SEC charges no fee.

### Labeled example: what PFIC status can cost a US investor

Editorial arithmetic on the Wilson Sonsini rate ceilings. Not tax advice; it ignores state tax, the net investment income tax, and the PFIC interest charge, which makes the gap larger.

| US investor's gain on exit | Long-term capital gains at 20% | Ordinary rates at 37% | Extra federal tax |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $1,000,000 | **$200,000** | **$370,000** | **$170,000** |
| $10,000,000 | **$2,000,000** | **$3,700,000** | **$1,700,000** |

That gap explains the covenant request. A fund that can make the QEF election with your annual statement avoids it, so give the fund the information it needs every year.

## Run the US raise

:::steps US raise readiness check
1. **Write your US angle in one sentence.** US customers, a US leader, a CEO move date, or why you beat the US options. Index: US investors usually expect a clear US angle.
2. **Decide lead or participant.** Pitch US leads only if you can show the angle. Otherwise build a local lead and fill a slice with a US fund that helps hiring or sales.
3. **Get structure advice first.** Ask US and home-country counsel whether you flip now, flip at the term sheet, or stay. See [Delaware flip](/library/fundraising/delaware-flip/).
4. **If you stay foreign, prepare the PFIC answer.** Cash management, an annual PFIC test, and a QEF statement the fund can file (Wilson Sonsini).
5. **Build the warm-intro map.** List the US firms your investors and portfolio founders can reach. Cold inbound is **10%** of deals (Gompers et al.).
6. **Warm the list before you raise.** Investor updates and coffee on each US trip (VEN session judgment).
7. **Batch the meetings.** EF advises arriving with **20+** conversations lined up and closing fast so the round does not go stale.
8. **Model the US hiring plan.** Index lists US hiring, customers, and partners as the payoff from a US investor. Show where the money lands.
:::

## Mistakes that cost the round

**Pitching US leads with no US angle.** A partner sees geography risk on top of early-stage risk and passes. Show US traction or a US move date first.

**Treating all US firms as equal.** A partner who will not fly to your board meetings is a weak lead. Index favors investors with a presence on both sides.

**Learning about PFIC from the term sheet.** A cash-rich foreign startup with US investors can trip the asset test in year two. Fix cash handling and the annual test before the money arrives.

**Moving the CEO before tax advice.** One founder's move can turn the company into a CFC and change the PFIC math for its smaller US holders (Wilson Sonsini).

**Forcing a small US check into a full round.** If the firm brings no hiring, customer, or partner help, the tax covenants and paperwork cost more than the check (VEN session judgment).
