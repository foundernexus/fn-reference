An IP assignment agreement moves ownership of code, designs, inventions, and other work from the person who made it to the company. Get one signed by every founder, employee, contractor, and advisor who builds anything. Founders sign two: an assignment of what they built before the company existed, usually inside the founder stock purchase agreement, and an ongoing confidential information and invention assignment agreement (CIIAA) for later work (Cooley GO). Employees sign a CIIAA before they start. Contractors sign an assignment in their consulting agreement, because a contractor's code usually is not a "work made for hire" and stays the contractor's until assigned in a signed writing (17 U.S.C. §§ 101, 204). Use present-tense words: "hereby assign". Not legal or tax advice.

:::takeaways
- Ownership starts with the person who made the work. The Supreme Court held in 2011 that an inventor must expressly grant rights to an employer (*Stanford v. Roche*).
- A copyright transfer is not valid unless it is in writing and signed by the owner (17 U.S.C. § 204(a)). A handshake with a friend who wrote the MVP transfers nothing.
- Commissioned work counts as made for hire only in **nine** listed categories, with a signed written agreement that says so (Copyright Office Circular 30). Custom software from a contractor needs an assignment.
- In California, an employee assignment clause cannot reach an invention built entirely on the employee's own time and equipment, unless it relates to the employer's business or comes from the employee's work (Labor Code § 2870). Delaware, Washington, and Minnesota have similar laws.
- Series A counsel asks for the assignment documents and the employee proprietary information agreements (YC Series A diligence checklist). A gap found after the term sheet slows a close that can already take more than a month.
:::

This page covers who signs which IP paper, the wording that works, state limits on employee assignments, and what Series A diligence asks to see. ## Who signs what

:::highlight
Every person who builds for the company signs before they build, or as soon as you find the gap. Founders first.
:::

| Person | Document | When | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Founder, work done before incorporation | IP assignment agreement, often inside the restricted stock purchase agreement | At formation, with the founder stock | Cooley GO incorporation package |
| Founder, ongoing work | CIIAA (or consulting agreement with an assignment if the founder is not an employee) | At formation | Cooley GO glossary |
| Employee | CIIAA, separate from the offer letter | Before the first day | Cooley GO; Cooley GO CIIAA guide |

Cooley GO's Delaware incorporation package generates the CIIAA for each founder and a restricted stock purchase agreement that includes an assignment agreement for intellectual property. Stripe Atlas uses the same form documents. If you incorporated without either, check what your founders signed.

## Why the signed paper matters

Patents start with the inventor. In *Stanford v. Roche* (2011), the Supreme Court said rights in an invention belong to the inventor, and an inventor must expressly grant them to an employer. In that case the Federal Circuit held that a researcher's "agree to assign" to Stanford lost to his later "do hereby assign" to another company, and the Supreme Court affirmed.

Copyright splits employees from contractors. A work prepared by an employee within the scope of employment is a work made for hire, and the employer owns it (17 U.S.C. § 101). A commissioned work from a contractor is made for hire only if all four of the Copyright Office's criteria hold: it falls in one of the **nine** listed categories, there is a written agreement, the agreement says the work is made for hire, and all parties signed it (Circular 30, revised August 2024). The nine include contributions to a collective work, parts of a motion picture, translations, compilations, and tests. Standalone software is not a named category, so the consulting agreement has to assign your contractor's code.

A transfer of copyright other than by operation of law needs a written instrument signed by the owner (17 U.S.C. § 204(a)). That is the whole case for collecting signatures before work starts.

## The wording: "hereby assign"

Cooley GO calls the present assignment the "magic words": the person hereby assigns rights to the company now and agrees to assign future inventions when they are made. Its sample reads "I hereby assign, and agree to assign in the future … to ABC Corp. all my right, title, and interest in any inventions made, conceived, reduced to practice, or learned by me". Porter Hedges makes the same point from the case law: "will assign and do hereby assign" conveys title in the present, while "agree to assign" is a promise for later.

Read every form you inherited. If an old contractor agreement says "agrees to assign" only, ask counsel about a new signed assignment.

## Founder IP from before the company

Most early code predates the company. A founder who wrote the prototype on a laptop before incorporation owns it until the founder assigns it. Put it in the founder stock paperwork at formation (Cooley GO).

Two founder risks show up in diligence:

- **Prior employer claims.** Your old employer's CIIAA may cover inventions that relate to its business, even ones you built at night. In California, § 2870 protects inventions built entirely on your own time without the employer's equipment, supplies, facilities, or trade secrets, unless they relate to the employer's business or research or result from your work there. Read your old agreement before you build on overlap.
- **Carve-outs.** Most CIIAAs let the signer list prior inventions to exclude. Cooley GO advises checking those lists for overlap with what the company plans to build, because investors and acquirers will, and taking a broad license to any prior invention the person uses in company work.

## State limits on employee assignments

Several states cap how far an employee CIIAA can reach. The wording below is each statute's.

| State | Statute | What the employee keeps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| California | Labor Code § 2870 | Inventions developed entirely on own time without employer equipment, supplies, facilities, or trade secret information, unless they relate to the employer's business or actual or demonstrably anticipated R&D, or result from work for the employer. A broader clause is unenforceable. |
| California | Labor Code § 2872 | For agreements entered into after January 1, 1980, the employer must give written notice of the § 2870 limit when the agreement is made. |
| Delaware | 19 Del. C. § 805 | Same structure: own time, no employer equipment, supplies, facility, or trade secret information, unless related to the employer's business or R&D, or from work for the employer. |
| Washington | RCW 49.44.140 | Same structure for inventions made without employer equipment, supplies, facilities, or trade secret information and developed entirely on the employee's own time. |
| Minnesota | Minn. Stat. § 181.78 | Same structure for inventions made without employer equipment, supplies, facility, or trade secret information and developed entirely on the employee's own time. |

Cooley GO's CIIAA form is for California employees. Use a form reviewed for each state where people work, and attach the state notice where the law requires one.

## What Series A diligence asks to see

YC's Series A diligence checklist, written by Jason Kwon as general counsel of YC Continuity, asks for a list of trademarks, patents, copyrights, and domain names, and for documentation of any assignment to the company and its recordation with the government office. Under material agreements it asks for licenses and agreements on proprietary rights, "including employee confidentiality and proprietary information agreements", and for any agreement with obligations over **$25,000**. YC says a data room ready before the term sheet can cut as much as a week off a close that can take more than a month.

Record patent assignments. An assignment is void against a later purchaser for value without notice unless it is recorded at the USPTO within **three months** of its date or before that later purchase (35 U.S.C. § 261).

:::steps IP assignment check before you raise
1. **List everyone who built anything.** Founders, employees, contractors, agencies, advisors, interns. Include people who left.
2. **Pull the signed paper for each.** Founder stock agreement with the IP assignment, CIIAA, consulting agreement. Unsigned drafts do not count.
3. **Read the verb.** Look for "hereby assign". Flag any form that only says "agrees to assign".
4. **Check founder pre-formation work.** Confirm each founder assigned the prototype, domains, and repos at formation.
5. **Read the carve-out lists.** Compare prior inventions against your product. Ask counsel about any overlap.
6. **Check old employers.** For each founder and early engineer, read the prior employer's invention agreement for overlap with your product.
7. **Match forms to states.** Use the right state form and notice for each employee (California § 2870 and § 2872 at minimum).
8. **Fix gaps now.** Get signed assignments from anyone missing, including former contractors, while they still answer email.
9. **Record patent assignments.** File with the USPTO within three months.
10. **Put it all in the data room.** One folder per person, ready before the term sheet.
:::

## Mistakes that cost the close

**Relying on an offer letter.** Cooley GO describes the CIIAA as a separate document from the offer letter. An offer letter without assignment language can leave patent rights with the employee (*Stanford v. Roche*).

**Calling contractor code "work for hire."** For commissioned work outside the nine categories, the label does not make the company the author (Circular 30). Add a present assignment.

**Skipping the friend who built the MVP.** A copyright transfer needs a signed writing (§ 204(a)). Find that person before diligence does.

**One national form.** A clause broader than § 2870 is unenforceable in California, and the employer must give the § 2872 notice. Other states have their own versions.

**Waiting for the term sheet.** YC's checklist asks for these documents after signature. Collecting them then costs closing time.
