A sales pipeline review is a weekly meeting where you check every open deal against what the buyer has confirmed, then decide which deals belong in this period's forecast. Ask four things of each deal. Does it look like deals you have won? What did the buyer say or do behind the close date? Is the next step booked with a date? Has someone with budget authority taken part? Move or cut deals that fail. In Ebsta and Pavilion's data on **4.2 million** opportunities, **31%** sat past their close date, and deals whose close date moved more than **3** times saw win rates fall **77%**. Then set pipeline coverage from your own win rate, not the 3x habit. A 3x target assumes you win about one qualified deal in three.

:::takeaways
- Run two separate jobs in the meeting. Help each deal past its blocker, then decide its forecast period on buyer evidence alone.
- Ask for the reason behind every close date. Gong ties pushes of **3** weeks or more to lost deal control, across **13,439** opportunities.
- Cut deals that skip steps. Ebsta found **29%** of opportunities skipped a stage, and those were **46%** less likely to close.
- Set coverage as one divided by your qualified win rate. **48%** of leaders in Norwest's 2025 survey plan for 3x, but its median stage rates multiply to about **19%**, which needs about 5x.
- Score the forecast against actuals each month. In Xactly's 2024 survey, **4 in 5** sales and finance leaders had missed a quarterly forecast in the past year.
:::

This page covers the weekly review once you have more deals than you can hold in your head, which for most founders means from the first few enterprise deals through the first sales hires. To define what a qualified deal is in the first place, read [customer development as your early sales funnel](/library/gtm/customer-development-sales-funnel/). To decide whether the process is ready for more sellers, read [is your sales process repeatable?](/library/gtm/repeatable-sales-process/)

## Run two jobs, in order

:::highlight
Working a deal and forecasting it are different decisions. Make them separately.
:::

Most early pipeline reviews blur two questions. The first is "what does this deal need next?" That is coaching: the missing stakeholder, the security review, the pricing question. The second is "does this revenue belong in this month or quarter?" That is a forecast call, and only buyer evidence should move it. VEN session judgment: keep the seller's quarter-end target and any discount offered to pull a deal in out of the forecast evidence ([VEN session](https://www.venexecs.com/?utm_source=founderdecisions&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=library&utm_content=sales-pipeline-review)). A deal can need a lot of help and still be a real deal. It can also look healthy and not belong in this quarter.

Run the forecast call last, after the deal discussion, so a good story about blockers does not set the number.

## Ask four questions of every open deal

:::highlight
Every forecast deal needs a written answer to each question. "Going well" is not an answer.
:::

| Question | Evidence that counts | Red flag | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Is it comparable to deals you won? | Same buyer type, problem, and deal size as past wins | First deal of its kind, or a segment you have never closed | Ebsta H1 2024 update: win rates **3.1x** higher on opportunities that matched the seller's ideal customer profile |
| What sits behind the close date? | A buyer event: budget cycle, renewal of the old tool, a go-live the buyer needs | The date is the end of your quarter | Ebsta: **31%** of opportunities were past their close date |
| Is the next step booked? | A dated meeting or action the buyer agreed to | "They'll get back to us" | Ebsta: top reps were **412%** more likely to have a next step defined |
| Has someone with power taken part? | The budget owner has met you or approved the evaluation | Only your champion has seen it | Ebsta: top reps were **489%** more likely to have the economic buyer engaged before the solution was presented |

Source: Ebsta and Pavilion, B2B Sales Benchmark Report 2024; Ebsta, 2024 B2B Sales Benchmarks H1 Update (first row).

The third and fourth questions overlap with champion testing. If your one contact cannot book the budget owner, read [internal champion in B2B sales](/library/gtm/internal-champion/) before the deal goes into commit.

## Read the reason behind the close date

:::highlight
A close date should come from the buyer's calendar. Ask what event sets it.
:::

| Signal | Figure | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opportunities past their close date | **31%** | Ebsta and Pavilion 2024 |
| Deals pushed back during the year | **44%** | Ebsta and Pavilion 2024 |
| Win rate when the close date moved more than **3** times | **77%** lower | Ebsta and Pavilion 2024 |
| Win rate when a deal slipped | **67%** lower, worst when delayed over **8** weeks | Ebsta and Pavilion 2024 |
| Qualification stage **50%** longer than average | **120%** more likely to slip | Ebsta and Pavilion 2024 |
| Close date pushed **3** weeks or more | A sign of lost deal control; win rates fall further when the push crosses into the next month or quarter | Gong Labs, **13,439** opportunities |

A moving date is not bad in itself. Gong found close dates on won deals moved **31%** more than on lost deals, because stale deals have nothing to update. The question is why it moved. A buyer who adds a security review and books it has moved the date for a reason. A buyer who stopped answering has not.

VEN session judgment on timing: find the customer's own deadline, the goal they must hit by a date, and check that the decision-maker cares before you put serious evaluation work into the deal. When a date moves, write down the old date, what the buyer confirmed or left open, and who owns the next action.

## Cut deals that skip steps or stall

:::highlight
A smaller, honest pipeline forecasts better than a big hopeful one.
:::

Ebsta found **29%** of opportunities skipped a stage, and those were **46%** less likely to close. Opportunities updated weekly were **17%** more likely to close won. Top reps were **366%** more likely to close out a deal at the discovery stage instead of carrying it.

Cut or park a deal when one of these holds:

- No buyer contact in the last two to three weeks and no meeting on the calendar.
- The close date has moved twice with no new buyer event behind it.
- It skipped from first call to proposal with no qualification on record.
- The only reason to keep it is the size of the number.

Parked deals go back to nurture with a note on what would reopen them. They come out of coverage math.

## Size coverage from your own win rate

:::highlight
Coverage is one divided by your win rate on the pipeline you count. 3x is only right at a one-in-three win rate.
:::

| Measure | Figure | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Leaders who plan for 3x qualified pipeline coverage | **48%** | Norwest 2025 (**177** leaders) |
| Plan for 4x / 5x / 2x | **31%** / **10%** / **7%** | Norwest 2025 |
| Median conversion, sales-qualified lead to opportunity | About **40%** | Norwest 2025 |
| Median conversion, opportunity to proposal | About **42%** | Norwest 2025 |
| Median conversion, proposal to win | About **45%** | Norwest 2025 |
| Early-stage companies (under $15M) with quota attainment below **60%** | Nearly **40%** | Norwest 2025 |

Coverage math is simple. If you win one qualified opportunity in three, 3x pipeline covers the target. Multiply Norwest's two median stage rates, **42%** and **45%**, and opportunity-to-win comes to about **19%** (our arithmetic; the medians come from different respondents, so treat it as a rough guide). At **19%**, you need about **5.3x** coverage, not 3x.

Example, illustrative: your target is $300,000 of new ARR this quarter. Over the last four quarters you created **40** qualified opportunities and won **8**, a **20%** win rate. Coverage is 1 ÷ 0.20 = **5x**, so you need about $1.5M of qualified pipeline at the start of the quarter, less what you create and close inside a typical quarter. Short sales cycles make that in-quarter share bigger. Norwest's 2025 chart shows average cycles from about **2** months under $25,000 ACV to about **9** months above $500,000.

There is no reliable public benchmark yet for pipeline coverage, slip rate, or forecast error at Seed to Series A. The figures above come from mixed-stage surveys and vendor datasets.

## Use forecast categories with your own rates

:::highlight
Default stage probabilities are placeholders. Replace them with your own stage-to-win rates once you have the deals.
:::

| Category | What it means | Example probability in Salesforce's sample mapping |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pipeline | Qualified, early, not in this period's number | 10% to 60% |
| Best case | Could close this period if the open items resolve | 70% to 75% |
| Commit | Buyer has confirmed the date, the budget, and the remaining steps | 90% |
| Closed | Signed | 100% |
| Omitted | Lost or parked; out of the forecast | 0% |

Source: Salesforce Help, opportunity stage to forecast category mappings.

Write your commit rule down. A workable early rule: a deal enters commit only when the budget owner has agreed the price, the remaining steps have dates, and the paper is with the buyer's legal team. Your forecast is then commit plus closed, with best case shown on its own line.

## Score the forecast every month

:::highlight
Write the number down before the period starts, then compare it with what closed.
:::

| Measure | Figure | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sales and finance leaders who missed a quarterly forecast in the past year | **4 in 5** | Xactly 2024 (**405** leaders) |
| Missed two or more times | More than half | Xactly 2024 |
| Sales leaders whose forecasts are usually off by **10%** or more | **52%** | Xactly 2024 |
| Finance leaders usually off by less than **9%** | **66%** | Xactly 2024 |
| Sales leaders and sellers with high confidence in forecast accuracy | **45%** | Gartner 2019 State of Sales Operations (n = **296**) |

Gartner's fix is plain: publish forecast against actual each month or quarter so people can see how accurate it is. Keep a one-line record per period: commit at the start, closed at the end, and the deals that slipped with the reason. After three or four periods you will know whether your commit line runs high or low.

## Example: a founder's Monday review

Illustrative, not data from any company. Eight open deals add up to $640,000. Two have a budget owner meeting booked and a date tied to the buyer's fiscal year; they go to commit at $150,000. Three have a booked next step but no budget owner yet; they go to best case. One moved its date for the third time with no new buyer event; you park it. Two have had no contact in four weeks; you drop them from the forecast. The board hears $150,000 commit plus $200,000 best case, not $640,000 of pipeline.

:::steps Run the weekly pipeline review
1. **Freeze the list.** Pull every open deal the day before. Sort by close date.
2. **Work the deals.** For each deal, name the blocker and the owner of the next step. Ten minutes on the largest, less on the rest.
3. **Ask the four questions.** Comparable to wins, buyer event behind the date, next step booked, power involved. Write the answers.
4. **Log every date change.** Old date, new date, what the buyer confirmed, who owns the next action.
5. **Cut or park.** No contact, repeat date moves with no buyer event, or skipped qualification.
6. **Set categories.** Apply your written commit rule. Best case stays on its own line.
7. **Check coverage.** Qualified pipeline divided by the remaining target, against one over your own win rate.
8. **Record the forecast.** Commit and best case for the period, dated.
9. **Score last period.** Commit at the start against closed at the end, with the slipped deals and their reasons.
:::

## Mistakes founders make in pipeline reviews

- **Counting conversations as pipeline.** Coverage on unqualified deals tells you nothing. Count qualified opportunities only.
- **Copying 3x.** At a **20%** win rate, 3x coverage misses the target by design.
- **Quarter-end close dates.** A date set by your calendar is a wish. Ask what the buyer's calendar says.
- **Discounting to pull a deal in.** Ebsta found win rates dropped **39%** when discounts came up before negotiation.
- **Forecasting on the story.** Decide the category after the coaching discussion, on evidence only.
- **Skipping the score.** Without a record of forecast against actual, you cannot tell whether the commit line runs high.
