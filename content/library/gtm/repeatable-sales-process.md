Your sales process is repeatable when five things hold at once. The deals you win come from one kind of buyer. They buy for one reason you can state in their words. You have written down the steps from first call to signature, and close rate and cycle time hold steady across comparable cohorts of qualified opportunities. New customers reach a first result on schedule. Someone other than a founder runs the written steps and closes. Until all five hold, a new sales hire runs experiments on salary. Mark Roberge, HubSpot's founding sales leader, sets the retention test as **P%** of new customers reaching a value event within a set time, and he often sees P set at **60–80%**. Once the tests pass, hire at a pace you can watch. His example is **2** salespeople a month for six months, not ten in January.

:::takeaways
- Read win rate by buyer type first. Ebsta found win rates **3.1x** higher on opportunities that matched the seller's ideal customer profile, and only **18%** of 2024 pipeline did.
- Count close rate as won deals over qualified opportunities, by cohort. A recent cohort that has not finished a sales cycle will look worse than it is (Stage 2 Capital).
- Test retention before you test sales capacity. Stage 2 Capital: write it as P% of customers reaching event E within time T, then check it against renewals (HubSpot used **80%** using **5** of **25** features within **60** days).
- A first rep is a slow, noisy test. The Bridge Group's 2026 survey of **158** B2B companies: average ramp **6.2** months, **48%** of reps at quota.
- Write the playbook before you hire into it, and scale as a pace. Leslie and Holloway (HBR, 2006): hiring a full sales force too fast burns cash and misses revenue targets.
:::

This page is the test between founder-led sales and a sales team. For who to hire and when the leader arrives, use [when to hire a VP of Sales](/library/hiring/when-to-hire-first-vp-sales/). For the stages of the funnel itself, use [customer development as your early sales funnel](/library/gtm/customer-development-sales-funnel/).

## Run five tests on the deals you already have

:::highlight
Pull your last 20 to 30 qualified opportunities and score them against these five rows before you open a sales requisition.
:::

| Test | What to check | Passing looks like | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Same buyer | Industry, size, and the title of the person who said yes on won deals | Most wins share one segment and one buyer role; losses cluster outside it | Ebsta: win rates **3.1x** higher on opportunities that match the profile |
| Same reason to buy | The problem and outcome the buyer named on the first call | You can write the reason in one sentence the buyer would sign | Stage 2 Capital: discovery and presentation guides built from the buyer's journey |
| Predictable steps | Close rate on qualified opportunities and days to close, by cohort | Mature cohorts land near each other; few deals skip a stage or slip | Ebsta: win rates fell **67%** on deals that slipped |
| Customers reach value | Share of new customers who hit a defined first result on time | P% of a cohort reaches event E within T; P often set at **60–80%** | Stage 2 Capital (Roberge) |
| Someone else closes | Deals a non-founder ran from first call to signature with the written steps | A hired or internal seller wins at a rate near the founder's | Stage 2 Capital; Leslie and Holloway |

The first two rows are about focus. Ebsta's H1 2024 update found that only **18%** of 2024 pipeline matched the seller's own ideal customer profile. If your won deals spread across five segments, a new rep will copy the spread and the close rate with it.

The VEN session framing matches the first row: start with the customers you can reach, win, and serve with the product you have today, and record why each opportunity qualifies or does not.

## Read close rate by cohort

:::highlight
Group qualified opportunities by the month or quarter they qualified. Judge a cohort only after it has had a full sales cycle to close.
:::

Close rate is won deals divided by qualified opportunities in the same cohort. Count only opportunities that passed your written qualification line. Track days from qualified to signed beside it.

Editorial example, not data from any company:

| Qualified in | Qualified opportunities | Won so far | Close rate so far |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Q1 | **20** | **5** | **25%** |
| Q2 | **24** | **6** | **25%** |
| Q3 | **22** | **3** | **14%** |

Q1 and Q2 match, which is a repeatability signal. Q3 looks like a collapse but has not finished its cycle. Roberge flags the same pattern: recent qualified opportunities have not gone through the sales cycle yet, so results from a few months ago are the better estimate. Wait before you call Q3 a problem, and do not average it into the board number.

The VEN session guidance runs the same way: judge close rate on qualified opportunities in comparable cohorts, and give a new cohort time to convert before you draw a conclusion.

## Test retention before sales capacity

:::highlight
A repeatable motion that sells to customers who leave is a fast way to spend the round.
:::

Roberge defines product-market fit by retention and argues most early retention problems start in sales and marketing: the customers you target and the promises made in the sale. Renewals take a year to show up, so he uses a leading indicator written as **P% of customers achieve event E within time T**.

| Company (Roberge's examples) | P | Event | Time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Slack | **70%** | **2,000+** team messages | **30** days |
| Dropbox | **85%** | Upload one file in one folder on one device | **1** hour |
| HubSpot | **80%** | Use **5** of **25** features | **60** days |

Pick an event that is binary, measurable in your product, and tied to the value you sell. Roberge sets T at one to three months for most software companies. If you sell six-figure contracts and close one or two a quarter, cohorts are too small. He suggests a health card per customer instead: setup status, active users, breadth of use, measured value, and executive willingness to be a reference.

Then check the indicator against reality. Roberge's method looks at customers acquired 12 to 18 months ago and compares retention for those who hit the event against those who did not. For the first-customer side of this work, use [how to keep your first customers](/library/gtm/early-customer-retention/).

## Know what a first rep costs while you find out

:::highlight
A hired seller takes months to produce a reading, and one seller's result cannot separate the person from the process.
:::

| Measure | Finding | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Average AE ramp to full productivity | **6.2** months, the highest in the study's history | The Bridge Group, 2026 (**158** B2B companies) |
| Share of reps at annual quota | **48%**, down from **51%** in 2024 | The Bridge Group, 2026 |
| Median AE on-target earnings | **$200K** | The Bridge Group, 2026 |
| Median AE quota | **$960K**, **4.6x** OTE | The Bridge Group, 2026 |
| Experience required at hire | **3.7** years | The Bridge Group, 2026 |
| Reps who missed quota | **69%** | Ebsta and Pavilion, 2024 (**530** companies, **4.2M** opportunities) |
| Revenue concentration | **17%** of reps generated **81%** of revenue | Ebsta and Pavilion, 2024 |

These surveys cover established sales teams, and The Bridge Group notes its sample skews toward companies with engaged revenue leadership. There is no reliable public benchmark yet for first-seller ramp or quota attainment at Seed to Series A. Read the table as the cost of the test, not as a target.

Editorial arithmetic: at the Bridge Group median of **$200K** OTE, six months of a seller at full OTE is about **$100K** before the first full-quota month. Roberge adds a **25%** buffer to direct seller cost for management and ramp when he models unit economics. One seller who misses tells you little, because the Ebsta spread shows how far individual results vary. Hire two so you can compare, as covered on [when to hire a VP of Sales](/library/hiring/when-to-hire-first-vp-sales/).

## Write the playbook before you hire into it

:::highlight
A seller from a large incumbent arrives used to training, a playbook, and a manager. Write the first two before the hire starts.
:::

Roberge's go-to-market playbook has five parts, built from the buyer's side:

| Part | What it holds |
| --- | --- |
| Buyer journey | The steps a buyer goes through, from naming the problem to using the product |
| Prospecting guide | How a seller earns the first meeting, with a message that fits where the buyer is |
| Discovery guide | One page of questions grouped by buyer stage, not a script |
| Presentation guide | Three to five demo paths, picked from what discovery found |
| Customer success guide | The onboarding that gets a new customer to the retention event |

Add retention checks to qualification: whether IT knows the setup work, whether end users took part in the purchase, and whether the buyer's systems fit the product. Roberge notes a seller can get a signature without these, and the customer may not succeed.

Then review real calls. Roberge runs "film reviews": the go-to-market team, the founder, and someone from product listen to a recorded discovery call together and agree on what to change. Follow local recording consent law.

The first hire is a different person at each stage. Roberge describes the product-market-fit hire as half product manager and half account executive, and the next hire as a process builder who writes the playbook down. He calls hiring a sales leader too early the first mistake most companies make here.

## Hire at a pace you can watch

:::highlight
Set a hiring rate, watch retention and unit economics each month, and speed up only while both hold.
:::

Leslie and Holloway call it the sales learning curve. Before a company can sell a new product efficiently, the whole organization has to learn how customers buy and use it. In their experience, hiring a full sales force before that burns cash and misses revenue expectations.

Roberge names front-loading sales hires at the start of the year as one of five common causes of missed targets in Series A companies. His pattern from companies he worked with: 10 salespeople hired in January, 2 left by December. His alternative is a pace: **2** a month for six months, then **4** a month if the indicators hold. His go-to-market fit targets are LTV to CAC above **3**, payback under **12** months, and a magic number above **1.0**. Check yours with the [magic number and CAC payback calculator](/tools/magic-number-cac-payback-calculator/).

## Run the repeatability check

:::highlight
Ten steps. Do them in order before the first sales requisition goes to the board.
:::

:::steps Repeatability check
1. **Write the qualification line.** The problem, the buyer's influence, and the blockers that disqualify a deal.
2. **Pull the qualified opportunities.** The last 20 to 30, with segment, buyer title, outcome, and days to close.
3. **Find the winning segment.** Compare close rate inside and outside it.
4. **Write the reason to buy.** One sentence in the buyer's words, checked against won-deal notes.
5. **Build cohorts.** Group by month or quarter of qualification, and judge only cohorts past one full cycle.
6. **Set the retention event.** P%, event, and time, measured on recent customer cohorts.
7. **Write the playbook.** Buyer journey, prospecting, discovery, demo paths, and onboarding.
8. **Hand off live deals.** Let a non-founder run some from first call to signature with the playbook.
9. **Compare results.** Their close rate and cycle against the founder's on the same segment.
10. **Set the hiring pace.** A monthly rate, the indicators you will watch, and the reading that pauses hiring.
:::

## Mistakes that cost a sales hire

:::highlight
Each one turns a hire into a guess.
:::

**Counting all pipeline.** Close rate on unqualified opportunities hides whether the motion works.

**Judging the newest cohort.** A quarter that has not finished its cycle looks weak.

**Selling to customers who will not stay.** A fast close on a buyer who does not reach the retention event shows up later as churn.

**Hiring one seller and reading the result.** Six months of ramp and a wide spread between reps leave one data point.

**Hiring the incumbent's top rep first.** They sold with training, a playbook, and a manager you do not have yet.

**Hiring ten at once after the round.** You cannot interview, onboard, and coach that many sellers well in the same month.
