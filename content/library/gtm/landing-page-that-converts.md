A startup landing page converts when a visitor can tell in a few seconds who it is for, what pain it removes, and what they get by clicking. Put those three things in the first screen: the audience in the headline, the pain in plain words, and a button that names the payoff, such as “Book a 20-minute pipeline review” in place of “Learn more.” Below that, show proof you can count and the product itself, then the price. Give the page one call to action. At Seed, many buyers arrive from a referral or an email you sent, so the page’s job is to keep the deal you already started. Nielsen Norman Group’s 2018 eyetracking study found that people spend about **57%** of their viewing time above the fold.

:::takeaways
- Write the first screen for one buyer. Name the role or industry, state the pain the way that buyer says it, and put the payoff on the button.
- Use proof you can count. Customer logos and an operating number from your product beat a satisfaction percentage.
- Show the product before the price, and publish a price or a starting point if you can. In TrustRadius’s 2022 survey of **2,185** technology buyers, **71%** said visible pricing would make them more likely to buy.
- Write at a plain reading level. In Unbounce’s 2024 SaaS data, pages written at a 5th to 7th grade level converted at **12.9%**, against **2.1%** for professional-level copy.
- Measure qualified demos by traffic source. There is no reliable public benchmark yet for visitor-to-qualified-demo conversion on a Seed or Series A B2B site.
:::

This page covers the homepage and campaign landing pages of a B2B startup selling to businesses. For an AI product deciding whether “AI” belongs in the headline, read [how to market an AI product without leading with AI](/library/gtm/market-ai-product-without-saying-ai/). For the funnel the demo requests enter, read [customer development as your early sales funnel](/library/gtm/customer-development-sales-funnel/).

## Decide what the page has to do

:::highlight
At Seed, warm channels start the deal. The site decides whether a warm visitor trusts you enough to take the next step.
:::

6sense’s 2024 buyer study found that B2B buyers are about **70%** of the way through their process before they contact a seller, and **81%** already have a preferred vendor at that first contact. Your page is part of that silent research. For an early company, many of those visitors come from a founder email, an intro, an event, or a post. They already know your name. They want to confirm the fit and find the next step.

Unbounce analyzed SaaS campaign landing pages from July 2023 to July 2024. Median conversion rates varied by traffic source:

| Traffic source | SaaS median conversion (Unbounce 2024) | What the page should do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Email | **16.9%** | Confirm what the email promised and take the click |
| Paid search | **4.1%** | Match the search words in the headline, then prove fit |
| Paid social | **2.9%** | Explain the problem fast to a visitor who was not looking |
| Display ads | **0.3%** | Treat it as awareness. Do not judge the page on it |

Unbounce counts any conversion event on its hosted pages, from a demo request to a download, so read these as a spread by channel. A demo request on your homepage is a different event.

The VEN session view: for a company whose first hundred customers came through founder intros and referrals, the website’s job was to avoid losing the deal. Sequence channels warm first, then cold and paid. Visitor identification tools that flag companies on your site help once traffic exists and do nothing before. Judgment from a [VEN session](https://www.venexecs.com/?utm_source=founderdecisions&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=library), not a survey.

## Give the first screen three jobs

:::highlight
Audience, pain, and payoff. Everything else on the first screen competes with them.
:::

Nielsen Norman Group tracked more than **130,000** eye fixations from **120** participants in 2018. About **57%** of viewing time went to the first screen and **74%** to the first two. Within the first screen, more than **65%** of viewing time went to the top half.

| Element | Write this | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Headline | The buyer and the outcome: “Month-end close in three days for multi-entity startups” | A category label or a vision line: “Finance, reimagined” |
| Subhead | The pain in the buyer’s words and how the product removes it | A list of features |
| Call to action | The payoff of the click: “Get a free close-time review” | “Learn more,” “Let’s talk,” or two equal buttons |
| Proof line | Two to five customer logos or one operating number | “Trusted by leading companies” with no names |
| Product image | A real screen showing the outcome, large enough to read | An abstract illustration or a tiny dashboard |

Editorial examples, not tested copy. Write the headline in the words customers use. Aaron Epstein, a YC group partner, suggests asking existing users how they would describe the product and reusing their phrasing. In YC’s design review with Pete Koomen, co-founder of Optimizely, the pair argue for one clear call to action and against elements that compete with it.

The VEN session view on the first screen: name the audience, lead with the specific pain, and make the button say what the visitor gets. The strongest example in the session was a bookkeeping startup whose real hook was the founder’s complaint about an accountant who stopped answering. That complaint made a better headline than the category. The same session pushed logos and hard operating numbers up the page: an average response time in minutes says more than a satisfaction percentage. Judgment from a [VEN session](https://www.venexecs.com/?utm_source=founderdecisions&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=library), not a survey.

## Write it the way your buyer reads

:::highlight
Plain words and short sentences converted better in Unbounce’s SaaS data.
:::

In Unbounce’s SaaS readability data, pages written at a 5th to 7th grade reading level converted at **12.9%**. Pages written at a professional level converted at **2.1%**. The best-converting SaaS pages ran **250** to **725** words.

Read the page aloud to someone outside your company. Mark each word they ask about. Replace it, or define it in the next sentence. Keep technical detail for the pages a technical buyer opens on purpose, such as docs, security, and integrations.

Test with strangers before you test with traffic. Ask five people from your target role to look at the first screen for five seconds, then ask what the company does and who it is for. If they cannot answer, rewrite the headline before you buy any ads.

## Use proof you can count

:::highlight
A buyer believes a named customer and a number from your product. A percentage with no source reads as decoration.
:::

| Proof | What it tells the buyer | Use it when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Customer logos | Companies like theirs pay you | You have permission and two or more logos from the target segment |
| Operating number | The product does the job at a measurable level | You can pull it from your own data, with the period and sample stated |
| Named customer quote | A peer had the same pain and it went away | The quote names the result and the person’s role |
| Short case study | How the outcome happened, step by step | A buyer needs a story to forward internally |
| Security and compliance page | You pass the review before procurement asks | You sell into companies with a security team |
| Satisfaction score | Little by itself | Only with a named survey, sample, and date |

TrustRadius found that **35%** of buyers said customer reviews on the vendor’s site would make them more likely to buy. Put proof under the first screen, and repeat one piece of it next to the call to action.

## Show the product, then the price

:::highlight
A price with no context reads as expensive. A price after the product reads as a decision.
:::

TrustRadius surveyed **2,185** technology buyers in February 2022. **81%** wanted to find pricing information on their own, and **54%** first looked for it during initial research. Asked what would make them more likely to buy, **71%** named pricing on the website and **70%** named access to a demo or free trial. Buyers who could not find a price split three ways: **43%** said they would delay and keep researching, **41%** would book a sales call to get it, and **16%** would stop considering the product.

| Pricing choice | Fits when | What to show |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Full public price | Self-serve or a simple plan with few variables | Plans, what each includes, and the charge metric |
| Starting price | Deals vary by seats, usage, or modules | “Plans from $X a month” plus what moves the number |
| Price after a short qualifier | Enterprise deals with a security review and custom terms | What the buyer gets on the call, how long it takes, and typical contract shape |

Put a product screen, a short recorded walkthrough, or a sandbox before the pricing section. For the charge metric itself, see [seat vs usage vs outcome pricing for AI SaaS](/library/gtm/seat-vs-usage-vs-outcome-pricing-ai-saas/).

## Keep one call to action and a short form

:::highlight
Each extra button or field is another place for the visitor to stop.
:::

| Weak button | Stronger button | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Learn more | See how the close works in 3 minutes | Names the payoff and the time cost |
| Contact us | Book a 20-minute workflow review | Says what happens on the call |
| Get started | Start a 14-day trial, no card | Removes the main risk |
| Request a demo | Get a demo on your own data | Promises their own problem on screen |

Editorial examples, not tested copy. Ask only for what you need to route the request: name, work email, company, and one question about the problem. Collect the rest on the call. Repeat the same call to action at the end of the page rather than adding a second one.

Unbounce found that **79%** of visits to SaaS landing pages came from mobile devices, with mobile and desktop average conversion rates close together (**6.4%** and **6.2%**). Check the first screen and the form on a phone before you ship.

## Measure qualified demos by source

:::highlight
Count the demos that turn into real conversations, and split them by where the visitor came from.
:::

| Metric | Definition | Use it to decide |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Visitor to call to action | Clicks or form submits ÷ unique visitors, by source | Whether the first screen works for that audience |
| Form completion | Submits ÷ form starts | Whether the form asks too much |
| Request to qualified demo | Requests that pass your written qualified line ÷ all requests | Whether the page attracts your buyer |
| Qualified demo to opportunity | Demos that end on a dated next step ÷ qualified demos | Whether the page set the right expectation |
| Time to first reply | Hours from request to your first answer | Whether follow-up loses warm buyers |

Editorial example, not a benchmark: a Seed company gets 2,000 visits in a month, half from founder email and intros. The page produces 50 demo requests, a 2.5% rate. 18 pass the qualified line and 7 end on a dated next step. The headline change worth testing is the one that raises the 18, not the 50.

For context only, Unbounce reported a SaaS median of **3.8%** across its hosted campaign pages and **11.6%** or higher for the top quarter. Those pages count any conversion and are not homepages. There is no reliable public benchmark yet for visitor-to-qualified-demo conversion on a Seed or Series A B2B site, so track your own by source for two or three months.

## Rewrite the page in order

:::highlight
Ten steps from the buyer you name to the number you track.
:::

:::steps Landing page rewrite
1. **Name one buyer.** The role or industry that pays you today.
2. **Collect their words.** Ask customers how they describe the product and the pain (Epstein).
3. **Write the headline.** Audience and outcome in one line.
4. **Write the button.** The payoff of the click and the time it takes.
5. **Pick the proof.** Logos and one operating number with its period.
6. **Show the product.** A real screen or a short walkthrough before the price.
7. **Decide the price display.** Full, starting at, or after a qualifier (TrustRadius).
8. **Cut the copy.** Plain words, **250** to **725** words on the page (Unbounce).
9. **Test with five strangers.** Five seconds on the first screen, then ask what it does.
10. **Track qualified demos.** By source, monthly, with the first reply time.
:::

## Mistakes that cost the warm visitor

:::highlight
Each one makes a buyer who already heard of you do extra work to say yes.
:::

**Leading with the category.** “Modern accounting” tells the buyer nothing about their problem. Name the pain.

**Writing for investors.** Market size and vision statements belong in the deck.

**Two equal buttons.** “Book a demo” beside “Start free” splits the click. Pick the one that matches your sales motion.

**Hiding the product.** Abstract art and stock photos hide what the buyer would use.

**A price wall with no context.** Show the outcome and the product first.

**Buying traffic before the page works.** Fix the first screen on warm traffic, then pay for cold.

**Judging the page on raw conversion rate.** Fifty unqualified requests cost more founder time than ten good ones.
