You can sell to the enterprise at Seed if three things hold. Your product does one important job far better than the vendor the buyer already trusts. The first deployment can stay inside one team. You can deliver what you promise within about **90** days of signing, which is Jason Lemkin’s test for too soon. Then run each deal on three tracks from the first call: the champion who wants it, the budget owner who signs, and the security and procurement teams who can stall it. Send your security packet before anyone asks. Forrester’s 2026 survey counts **13** internal stakeholders in a typical buying decision, and procurement acts as a decision-maker in **53%** of buying cycles.

:::takeaways
- Check fit before you chase logos. Lemkin: a big company takes a risk on a startup when one important thing is **10x** better and the risk stays contained. It is too soon if you cannot support the customer **90** days after close.
- Separate the champion from the budget owner. Ask whether the purchase is budgeted (Lemkin). Forrester counts **13** internal stakeholders and **9** outside influencers per decision.
- Lead with security proof. In Vanta’s 2025 survey, **77%** of businesses say stakeholders demand verified proof of compliance, up from **65%** in 2024. A first SOC 2 Type 2 needs an observation window of at least three months, so start it when the first enterprise deal enters the pipeline.
- Ask how they buy on the first serious call and run procurement in parallel (YC’s Pete Koomen). Norwest’s 2025 survey puts average cycles at about **2–3** months under **$25K** ACV and about **9–12** months above **$500K**.
- Set a stall rule. Lost deals took **225** days against **115** for won deals in the Fullcast and Pavilion 2026 data.
:::

This page covers the enterprise motion from account list to signature. If the buyer wants proof first, read [how to convert a pilot to a paid contract](/library/gtm/pilot-to-paid-contract/). For the paper at the end, read [how to negotiate your first customer contract](/library/gtm/first-customer-contract/).

## Decide whether enterprise is too early

:::highlight
Enterprise readiness is a test of the product, the team, and the runway. ARR alone does not answer it.
:::

| Signal | Ready | Too early |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Product edge | One important workflow **10x** better than the incumbent (Lemkin) | A cheaper or slicker copy of a tool they already trust |
| Blast radius | One team or workflow can try it without risk to the core business | It needs them to rip out a system of record |
| Delivery | You can ship promised features within **90** days of close (Lemkin) | Roadmap promises you cannot staff |
| Who sells | A founder runs the deals (Koomen) | You expect a first sales hire to find the motion |
| Security | A written security packet now, SOC 2 started or scheduled | No clear answer on where data lives or who can access it |
| Deployment | Install is simple, or you budget hands-on install time per deal | You assume their IT team will set it up alone |
| Runway | Cash covers the cycle for your ACV band (Norwest) | One large deal carries the plan and the runway ends first |

Lemkin’s read of large buyers: at least **90%** of the time they pick the obvious, trusted vendor. Innovation groups bring in **1–2** new vendors a year that do not put the core business at risk. Startups still close Fortune 500 and Global 2000 customers in their first **1–12** months. They solve a specific problem the incumbents do not solve well, start small, and stay honest about team size.

The VEN session view on deployment: if the install needs skills the buyer lacks, make it simple or plan the hands-on hours into each deal. Pretending otherwise stretches the cycle. Judgment from a [VEN session](https://www.venexecs.com/?utm_source=founderdecisions&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=library), not a survey.

## Map the champion, the budget owner, and the blockers

:::highlight
The champion wants you to win. Someone else controls the money. Confirm both before you forecast the deal.
:::

Forrester’s 2026 buyer survey counts **13** internal stakeholders and **9** outside influencers in a typical decision. Purchases with generative AI features average **14** members against **7** without them. 6sense’s 2024 buyer study found an average buying group of **11** people, and buyers about **70%** of the way through before they contact a seller. **81%** already had a preferred vendor at that first contact.

Gartner found buyers spend **17%** of a purchase journey meeting potential suppliers. If they compare several vendors, any one rep gets about **5–6%**. Your champion makes the case in the other meetings.

| Role | What they decide | What you hand them |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Champion | Whether to spend political capital on you | Pricing one-pager, customer evidence, security packet, answers to objections |
| Budget owner | Whether money exists or moves from another line | A business case in cost, risk, or revenue |
| Technical staff | Whether it works in their environment | Architecture, integration steps, install plan |
| Security | Whether the data risk is acceptable | Security packet, SOC 2 status with dates, questionnaire answers |
| Procurement | Vendor setup, paper, and price | Order form, MSA, insurance certificate, vendor forms |
| Legal | Contract terms | Your standard MSA and data terms |

Ask the champion: is this budgeted? Lemkin: if it is, ask who else they are looking at. If it is not, you can still win from a smaller discretionary budget, so cut the first ask to fit.

VEN session judgment on reaching the group: a cold note to the CIO goes unanswered. Sell into the business functions the CIO serves, and win the technical staff who will deploy and run the product. Treat support, influence, and spending authority as three facts to confirm. A senior title or an enthusiastic call does not prove budget authority.

## Send security proof before they ask

:::highlight
Write one document that explains how you run the system. Send it with the first follow-up.
:::

Buyers now expect proof early. In Vanta’s 2025 State of Trust survey of **2,500** business and IT leaders, **77%** said stakeholders demand verified proof of compliance, up from **65%** in 2024. The same teams spend about **7** hours a week, or **9** working weeks a year, on vendor security reviews. Vanta puts a single security questionnaire at **5–15** hours of work, and standard formats such as CAIQ and SIG run to hundreds of questions.

The VEN session view: model the packet on the public security pages of large software companies. Describe where you store data, who can access it, how you handle an incident, and what you do with customer data in AI features. Evidence of how you operate carries more weight than a row of certification logos.

| Your situation | Move | Timing (source) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No enterprise deal in the pipeline | Write the security packet. Waiting on the audit is defensible | Workstreet |
| First enterprise deal in the pipeline | Start SOC 2 Type 2 readiness on the Security criterion only | First Type 2 in about **4–6** months; window of at least **3** months (Vanta) |
| A ready buyer asks for a report you lack | SOC 2 Type 1 as a bridge, then Type 2 | Type 1 in **3–5** months from scratch, **10–12** weeks with controls in place (Vanta) |
| An RFP requires Type 2 and you hold Type 1 | Check whether you can bid with Type 1 and a dated Type 2 commitment | Workstreet |
| Budgeting the first year | Scope the first audit to Security plus what contracts require | **$20,000–$80,000+** all-in (Vanta) |

Workstreet’s founder-side warning: a prospect that sends a security questionnaire or asks for a SOC 2 report means you are already a bit late. Map the Type 2 timeline to the quarter your first enterprise deal could close.

## Run security and procurement in parallel

:::highlight
Ask how they buy on the first serious call. Work that list alongside the evaluation.
:::

Pete Koomen, who co-founded Optimizely, describes closing as security, legal, and compliance reviews that can take weeks or months. His advice in YC’s Startup School: ask the prospect about each procurement step up front, and fill in the security questionnaire in parallel with the evaluation.

**Plan the cycle by ACV.** Norwest’s 2025 survey of **177** B2B revenue leaders found average sales cycles of about **2–3** months for deals under **$25K** ACV and about **9–12** months above **$500K**. There is no reliable public benchmark yet for enterprise sales cycles or security review time at Seed to Series A startups.

**List the procurement steps.** Vendor registration or portal, tax forms, insurance certificate, security review, MSA and data terms, order form, and a purchase order number. Name an owner on their side for each and a date.

**Size the first deal to the path.** VEN session judgment: a first deal that sits under the buyer’s approval threshold skips a sign-off and earns the larger contract from inside. Lemkin’s version: let big companies start smaller, with a paid pilot in a less risky workflow.

**Use a stall rule.** Fullcast and Pavilion’s 2026 benchmark of **361,000** opportunities across **316** companies found lost deals took **225** days against **115** for won deals. If a deal runs well past your won-deal cycle with no new stakeholder or date, take it out of the forecast and go back to the budget owner.

## Run the enterprise deal in order

:::highlight
Ten steps from account list to signature. The founder runs each one until the motion repeats.
:::

:::steps Enterprise deal
1. **Pick accounts where one workflow hurts.** Keep the list short. Lemkin’s advice: research each buyer and spend half an hour writing each email.
2. **Check fit before the meeting.** Confirm the product fits their environment before the first call (VEN session).
3. **Put price on page one.** Price, what they get, and how to trial it come before the founding story (VEN session).
4. **Qualify the problem, budget, and authority.** Koomen: ask questions before you pitch. Ask whether it is budgeted (Lemkin).
5. **Map the buying group.** Name the champion, the budget owner, the technical staff, and each blocker.
6. **Send the security packet.** Attach it to the first follow-up, with SOC 2 status and dates.
7. **Get the procurement path in writing.** Steps, owners, and dates. Start them the same week.
8. **Contain the first deployment.** One team or workflow, with a [paid pilot](/library/gtm/pilot-to-paid-contract/) if they need proof.
9. **Arm the champion.** One-pager, evidence, security packet, and objection answers for the rooms you are not in.
10. **Paper it and run the rollout.** List price with a dated discount ([first customer contract](/library/gtm/first-customer-contract/)). Koomen: the founder project-manages implementation.
:::

## Mistakes that stall enterprise deals

:::highlight
Each one adds months or kills the deal in a room you are not in.
:::

**Treating the champion as the buyer.** Your contact may care a great deal and still lack a budget line.

**Pitching the CIO cold.** Start with the functions the CIO serves and the people who will run the product (VEN session).

**Bluffing a capability.** A claim that fails in technical evaluation kills the deal. Saying you do not do something, and naming a vendor who does, builds credibility for the next one (VEN session).

**Waiting for the questionnaire to start SOC 2.** A first Type 2 report needs an observation window of at least **3** months (Vanta).

**Hiring a seller to find the first enterprise customer.** Koomen: before product-market fit, you are probably the only person who can sell the product.

**Keeping a stalled deal in the forecast.** Lost deals took about twice as long as won deals in the Fullcast and Pavilion data.
