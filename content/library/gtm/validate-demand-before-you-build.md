Before you write production code, write the demand bet as a number: at least X% of a named group will take a paid action at a stated price, by a date. Alberto Savoia calls this the XYZ Hypothesis. Then interview buyers about the last time the problem hit them, with your idea kept out of the call. Abbie Griffin and John Hauser found that **20–30** interviews in one segment surface **90%** or more of customer needs. Ask each strong prospect for something that costs them: a next meeting, an intro to the budget owner, or money. Write the kill line before you run the test, and build when paid commitments clear it. In CB Insights’ 2026 review of **431** VC-backed shutdowns, poor product-market fit appears in **43%** of the **385** cases with a known cause.

:::takeaways
- Say it with numbers. Savoia’s format reads “At least X% of Y will do Z.” Put a price and a deadline inside Z. His Law of Market Failure puts the failure rate for ideas that reach the market at **80–90%**.
- Interview about the past. Ask about the last time the problem happened and what they tried (YC’s Eric Migicovsky and Gustaf Alströmer). Ask whether they pay for another fix today. Plan **20–30** calls per segment (Griffin and Hauser).
- Count commitments. Rob Fitzpatrick: a compliment costs the buyer nothing and carries no data. Time, reputation, and money do.
- Run the cheapest test that asks for money: a priced fake door, a demo video, a service you run by hand, a deposit, or a paid pilot. Dropbox’s demo video took the beta waiting list from **5,000** to **75,000** overnight.
- Write the kill line before the test. There is no reliable public benchmark yet for waitlist or LOI conversion to paid, so the threshold comes from your own revenue model.
:::

This page covers the work before the first line of production code. ## Rank demand signals by what they cost the buyer

:::highlight
A signal counts in proportion to what the buyer gives up. Rank the signals before you count them.
:::

| Signal | What the buyer gives up | What it proves | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Compliment or “I would buy that” | Nothing | Nothing. Read it as a polite exit | Fitzpatrick; Alströmer |
| Survey answer | A few minutes | Stated interest, shaped by your question | Alströmer |
| Email or waitlist signup | An email address | Initial interest in the offer as written | Savoia; Dropbox |
| Next meeting with a set agenda | Time | The problem matters enough to spend more time on it | Fitzpatrick |
| Intro to a peer, the team, or the boss | Reputation | Your contact will stake standing on it | Fitzpatrick |
| Letter of intent | A signature with no legal obligation | Intent at a price, if the price is on the page | Fitzpatrick |
| Pre-order or deposit | Money | Willingness to pay before delivery | Fitzpatrick; Savoia |
| Paid pilot | Money, time, and reputation | Willingness to pay plus an owner inside the buyer | Fitzpatrick |

Fitzpatrick’s rule of thumb in The Mom Test: “The more they’re giving up, the more seriously you can take what they’re saying.” Alströmer, a YC group partner, says a five-minute video interview teaches you more than **500** or **5,000** survey responses. Paul Graham describes the trap with a made-up social network for pet owners: friends say “Yeah, maybe I could see using something like that,” and summed across the population that reaction gives you zero users.

The FounderNexus session view: money is the signal, and interviews or surveys count for little on their own. While you test, state the full price in writing and offer the early buyer a lower test price. Their reaction to the full number is the data. Judgment from a [FounderNexus session](https://www.foundernexus.com?utm_source=founderdecisions&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=library), not a survey.

## Write the bet as a number first

:::highlight
One sentence with a percentage, a named group, an action, and a price. The team agrees on it before the first call.
:::

Savoia, Google’s first engineering director and the author of The Right It, wrote the format on a Stanford whiteboard in 2016: “At least X % of Y will do Z.” X is a share of the target market, Y names the market, and Z is the action you expect. His students’ version: “At least 10% of people who live in cities with an AQI level greater than 100 will buy a $120 portable pollution sensor.” Writing the number exposed a disagreement. One student priced the device at **$200** and another at **$80** to **$100**.

Then zoom in. Savoia’s tactic is to think globally and test locally: turn the market-wide bet into a smaller one you can test this month on people you can reach.

| Part | Market-wide bet | Local test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| X | Share of the market that must act | Share of the people you contact |
| Y | The full segment | The segment members inside your network or one channel |
| Z | Buys at the target price | Pays a deposit, signs a priced pilot, or books a second call with the budget owner |

Editorial example, not data: “At least 10% of revenue operations leads at B2B SaaS companies with 50 to 500 employees will pay $2,000 a month for renewal forecasting within 60 days of a first demo.” The local version: “At least 3 of the 30 revenue operations leads we can reach this month will sign a paid 60-day pilot at that price.”

FounderNexus session judgment on Z: for long sales cycles, put a time bound in the sentence. A prospect who takes months to answer fails the bet on time even if the interest sounds real. Test demand first. Whether you can build it, and at what cost, comes after.

## Run problem interviews before you show the product

:::highlight
Ask about the last time the problem happened. Keep your idea out of the call.
:::

Migicovsky, who founded Pebble, built his Startup School lecture on The Mom Test’s three rules: talk about their life instead of your idea, ask for specifics instead of hypotheticals, and listen more than you talk. Alströmer adds that you should introduce the idea at the end of the call or not at all.

| Ask | Avoid |
| --- | --- |
| Tell me about the last time that happened. | Would you use a product that did this? |
| What is the hardest part of doing it today? | Which features would make it better? |
| What have you tried, and what don’t you love about it? | What would a better product look like to you? |
| How often does it happen, and what does it cost you? | Questions with a yes or no answer |
| Who has the budget and the authority to fix it? | Two questions in one breath |

The left column comes from Migicovsky’s five questions and his prototype-stage checks on cost, frequency, and budget. The right column is Alströmer’s list of questions that derail an interview.

**Talk to earlyvangelists.** Steve Blank’s profile of the buyer who takes a risk on an unfinished product: they have the problem, know they have it, search for a fix on a timetable, have built a workaround, and have or can get the money. Alströmer’s test for value: are people paying for other solutions today? Spreadsheets compete with hundreds of startups, so a new product has to beat them by a wide margin.

**Plan the count.** Griffin and Hauser’s 1993 Marketing Science study concluded that **20–30** interviews should identify **90%** or more of customer needs in a relatively homogeneous segment. Alströmer moves to an MVP after **5** or **10** interviews. Use the small number to find the segment and the larger one to map its needs before you commit an engineering team.

**Run the calls yourself.** Migicovsky: talking to customers is part of the CEO’s job description, and the engineers take part too. FounderNexus session judgment: score each problem on how much it matters to the buyer and how satisfied they are with today’s fix. Look for high importance, low satisfaction, and a short path to money made, money saved, or risk reduced. Book the next conversation before the call ends.

## Pick the cheapest test that asks for commitment

:::highlight
Match the test to the risk. Each test ends in a buyer action you can count.
:::

| Test | What you build | Fits when | Example (source) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fake door | A page, button, or ad with the offer and price | Self-serve offers with traffic you can reach | McSpaghetti on the menu before any pasta exists (Savoia) |
| Demo video plus waitlist | A short screen recording of the intended product | The product is hard to build and the audience gathers in one place | Dropbox: **5,000** to **75,000** on the waiting list overnight (Houston, via Eric Ries) |
| Service run by hand | People do the work behind a plain front end | The output matters more than the automation | IBM tested speech-to-text with a hidden typist (Savoia) |
| Deposit or pre-order | An order form and a payment link | Hardware or long-lead products | Tesla asked interested buyers for a **$5,000** deposit (Savoia) |
| Short-run offer | A real, limited offer for a few days | Services and marketplaces | Airbnb: **3** guests and **$240** on the first night (Savoia) |
| Priced pilot or LOI | Pilot paper or an LOI with price, scope, and dates | B2B buyers with a budget owner | [Pilot to paid contract](/library/gtm/pilot-to-paid-contract/) |

Savoia’s note on fake doors: tell visitors at once that the product is not ready, and thank them with a gift close to what they tried to buy. Paul Buchheit’s advice, quoted by Graham, is to “go sell the product ASAP (before wasting time building it).” Founders who do learn whether anyone wants it, and often find the better idea along the way.

FounderNexus session judgment: the cheapest artifact today is often an AI-generated demo built in hours. Put it in front of target buyers and collect your own first-hand data. Industry reports and your own expertise do not count as that data.

## Set the kill line before you test

:::highlight
Write the pass line, the kill line, and the date. Read the result against them, and leave them where they are.
:::

Savoia names three paths for an idea: Drop It, Go for It, or Test It. He warns against the first two when you have no data. After the test, read the result against the bet you wrote.

| Result against the bet | Move |
| --- | --- |
| Paid commitments meet or beat the X you wrote | Build the smallest version for the buyers who paid |
| Strong pain, little money | Find who owns the budget, or retest at another price |
| Pull from one sub-segment only | Narrow Y and rerun the local test |
| Compliments, no commitments | Treat it as a no. Change the buyer or the problem |
| Interest that takes months to answer | Treat it as a no on the time bound |

There is no reliable public benchmark yet for the share of waitlist signups, LOIs, or fake-door clicks that become paying customers. Set X from your own revenue model and write it down before the first test.

Two more checks from the FounderNexus session. First, find the companies that tried this and died. Call their founders, ask what killed the company, and solve each of those flaws before you build. Second, walk a product-free deck past a few investors with no ask attached. They raise the hard questions early and often name predecessors you missed. Treat their take as data on how the story lands, filtered through each investor’s bias. A design partner who pays nothing does not count as validation, because investors read it as an unfinished product looking for a buyer.

## Validate demand in order

:::highlight
Ten steps from a written bet to the first build. Each step ends with evidence you can show a skeptic.
:::

:::steps Demand validation
1. **Write the XYZ bet.** Percentage, named group, paid action, price, and a date (Savoia).
2. **Zoom to a local test.** People you can reach this month through your network or one channel.
3. **List earlyvangelists.** Buyers with the problem, a workaround, a timetable, and money (Blank).
4. **Run problem interviews.** Past behavior only. Plan **20–30** in the segment (Griffin and Hauser).
5. **Score the pain.** Importance, satisfaction with today’s fix, and the path to money (FounderNexus session).
6. **Study the predecessors.** Call founders of companies that died on this problem (FounderNexus session).
7. **Build the cheapest artifact.** A priced fake door, a demo, or a service you run by hand.
8. **Ask for a commitment.** Time, an intro, or money, with the full price stated (Fitzpatrick).
9. **Read the result against the kill line.** Keep, narrow, reprice, or drop, as written before the test.
10. **Build for the buyers who paid.** Then measure pull with the Sean Ellis survey.
:::

## After you build, measure pull

:::highlight
Pre-build tests show willingness to pay. A survey of real users shows whether the product holds them.
:::

Sean Ellis’s question: “How would you feel if you could no longer use the product?” After benchmarking nearly a hundred startups, he found that companies with strong traction almost always had more than **40%** of users answer “very disappointed.” Migicovsky uses the same **40%** line in his lecture. Superhuman started at **22%** in summer 2017, reached **33%** after segmenting to its most enthusiastic users, and hit **58%** within three quarters, according to founder Rahul Vohra.

## Mistakes that waste the build

:::highlight
Each one makes a weak test read as a pass.
:::

**Counting compliments.** A meeting that ends with praise and no next step is a failed meeting (Fitzpatrick).

**Pitching on a discovery call.** Your idea biases the answers. Save it for the end of the call (Alströmer).

**Asking about the future.** “Would you pay for this?” gets a yes that means nothing. Ask what they paid for last time.

**Testing without a price.** A signup with no price tests curiosity. Put the dollar figure in Z.

**Delegating the calls.** The founder who will make the build decision runs the interviews (Migicovsky).

**Moving the kill line.** Rewriting the pass line after the result turns the test into a pitch.

**Calling an unpaid design partner validation.** Ask design partners to pay something (FounderNexus session).
