A conference produces customers when you treat it as a short sales sprint with a list, a script, and a scorecard. Write down the outcome before you buy a ticket: a named account met, a discovery call booked, or a buying process learned. Build a list of target people from the speaker and sponsor pages and your own network, and book meetings before you fly. End each conversation on one specific next step with a date. Follow up within a day with the thing you promised. Count qualified opportunities against the full cost of the trip, and ignore the badge-scan total. Freeman’s 2025 survey of **1,022** event attendees found that **58%** came to discover new products and only **6%** came to make a purchase decision.

:::takeaways
- Pick the outcome first. A big audience can still be the wrong audience for your current buyer.
- Book the meetings before the event. Speaker lists, sponsor lists, and warm intros give you names. A sponsor logo tells you a company paid, and says nothing about whether your buyer will be in the room.
- Send someone who knows the problem. In Freeman’s data, **58%** of attendees want time with subject matter experts, while **49%** of exhibitors think scanning badges and emailing afterwards would help most.
- Leave each conversation with a dated next step and an owner. A scan without a next step belongs on a nurture list.
- Score the event on qualified opportunities per dollar and per founder day. There is no reliable public benchmark yet for conference lead-to-opportunity conversion at Seed or Series A.
:::

This page covers in-person B2B events, from the decision to attend through the opportunities that follow. ## Decide what the event should produce

:::highlight
Write the outcome and the budget on one line before anyone books travel.
:::

Start from the buyer. Ask which roles and accounts you need to reach this quarter, and whether this event puts them in one place. Then choose an outcome you can recognize on the flight home.

| Outcome | You will know it happened when | Pick an event where |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Learn how a role buys | You have notes from conversations with people in that role at target accounts | The agenda targets that role, with sessions on their daily work |
| Meet named accounts | You held meetings with people at accounts on your list | The speaker and sponsor pages show those companies attending |
| Book discovery calls | Prospects agreed to a call with a date on the calendar | Practitioners with the problem attend, beyond vendors and investors |
| Recruit design partners | A team agreed to test a scoped version with you | Your segment is small and gathers in one place each year |
| Support an open deal | A champion introduced you to a colleague who signs or blocks | Your open accounts send people to it |

Set the budget in money and founder days. Count tickets, travel, prep time, and the follow-up hours after the event. Compare that total with other ways to reach the same people, such as warm intros or a small dinner you host.

The CMO Council and the Exhibit & Event Marketers Association surveyed more than **260** brand marketers in late 2012 and early 2013. **40%** were cutting back on big shows in favor of more targeted gatherings, **44%** were choosing to host their own events, and **19%** lacked a strategy to act on the leads they gathered.

## Attend, speak, host, or sponsor

:::highlight
Pick the format where you have something useful to say and your buyers have a reason to show up.
:::

| Format | What you get | When it fits a Seed or Series A team |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Attend with a target list | Hallway and pre-booked meetings | Default for a first visit to an event |
| Speak or teach a session | A room of people who chose your topic | You hold a clear view on the buyer’s work and can teach it without a pitch |
| Host a small side gathering | A dinner, breakfast, or roundtable with people you invited | Your list has enough named accounts to fill a table |
| Sponsor a booth | Floor traffic, badge scans, and the organizer’s promotion | You can staff it with people who know the problem, and the package includes meetings or a speaking slot |

Rob Fitzpatrick, in The Mom Test, argues that organizing your own event costs only a little more effort than attending one. The organizer gets credibility for sending the invites, and five or ten of the right people beat a large headcount. He makes the same case for teaching at conferences and workshops: you refine the message in front of serious prospects, then talk to the most interested attendees afterwards.

Freeman asked attendees and exhibitors which factors most help commercial conversations at events. Exhibitors rate pre-set meetings higher than attendees do (**41%** against **20%**). Attendees rate product demonstrations higher than exhibitors do (**63%** against **34%**). Book meetings anyway, and spend them showing the product on the attendee’s own problem.

The VEN session view: judge a sponsorship package by the meetings and follow-up work it buys, and by who on your team will do that work. A paid booth without a follow-up owner produces a spreadsheet. Judgment from a [VEN session](https://www.venexecs.com/?utm_source=founderdecisions&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=library), not a survey.

## Build the list before you fly

:::highlight
Keep the list short enough that you can prepare a reason to talk to each person.
:::

Use an attendee list if the organizer offers one under its terms. Without one, work from the speaker page, the sponsor page, your own network, and public posts from people saying they will attend. Treat a sponsor logo as a research lead.

For each priority contact, write four lines:

1. The problem you think they have, and why.
2. The facts you know about their team, and the guesses.
3. The reason a short meeting would help them.
4. The person who could introduce you, if anyone.

Ask for introductions where a real shared connection exists. Send meeting requests a week or two before the event with a specific time and place. If someone cannot meet, ask who on their team is attending.

## Run a short conversation that ends on a next step

:::highlight
Learn their problem, show the part you solve, and agree what happens next.
:::

Freeman’s attendees told exhibitors what they want. **58%** said speaking with subject matter experts from exhibiting companies would improve their interactions, against **26%** of exhibitors. **23%** of attendees picked being scanned and emailed afterwards, against **49%** of exhibitors. Staff the conversation with a founder or engineer who can talk about the buyer’s work, and keep the scanner for logistics.

Keep the first conversation short. Ask what they are working on and how they handle the problem today. Show the part of the product that answers it, in their words. Skip the full tour.

Freeman also found that **32%** of attendees were final decision-makers and **68%** had significant or some influence over purchases. Ask who else takes part in the decision before you leave.

| Next step | Ask for it when | Write down |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Discovery call | The problem fits and they own it | Date, attendees, and the question they want answered |
| Introduction to a colleague | They feel the problem and someone else decides | The colleague’s role and the reason for the intro |
| A promised resource | They want proof before a call | What you will send, and by when |
| Visit or demo at their office | An open deal needs the wider team | Who attends and what they need to see |
| Nurture | Fit is unclear or timing is far off | The trigger that would make a call worth it |

## Follow up within a day

:::highlight
Send the follow-up while they still remember the conversation.
:::

Speed matters for inbound leads. James Oldroyd, Kristina McElheran, and David Elkington audited **2,241** US companies’ responses to web leads for Harvard Business Review in 2011. Firms that tried to contact a lead within an hour were nearly **7** times as likely to qualify it as firms that waited even an hour longer. Against firms that waited **24** hours or more, the gap was more than **60** times. **23%** of the companies never responded. Those were web inquiries. There is no reliable public benchmark yet for follow-up speed on conference leads, so treat the study as a direction and set your own rule.

Write the follow-up around the conversation:

1. Name the problem or priority they mentioned.
2. Attach or link the thing you promised.
3. Restate the next step you agreed and offer a specific time.

Editorial template, not a tested sequence:

> Subject: [Their topic] from [event]
>
> You mentioned [their problem or priority]. Here is the [resource] I said I would send: [link].
>
> We talked about [next step]. Does [day and time] still work?

Send it from the founder who held the conversation. Follow the event’s data terms and your own privacy notice for anyone you scanned. Plan two or three relevant follow-ups, then move the contact to nurture. Stop when someone declines or asks you to.

The VEN session view on follow-up: carry the actual conversation into the email, and continue an existing thread when it helps the person place you. Judgment from a [VEN session](https://www.venexecs.com/?utm_source=founderdecisions&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=library), not a survey.

## Measure meetings and opportunities

:::highlight
Badge scans count activity. Qualified opportunities count results.
:::

| Metric | Definition | Use it to decide |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Target conversations | Conversations with people on your list or in your target role | Whether the event drew your buyer |
| Agreed next steps | Conversations that ended with a dated next step and an owner | Whether your script works |
| Next steps held | Agreed steps that happened within 30 days | Whether follow-up works |
| Qualified opportunities | Deals that pass your written qualified line | Whether the event fed the pipeline |
| Cost per qualified opportunity | All-in cost ÷ qualified opportunities | Whether to return next year |

Editorial example, not a benchmark: two founders spend $9,000 all-in on a two-day event. They scan 40 badges, hold 14 conversations with target accounts, agree 8 next steps, hold 5 of them, and qualify 2 opportunities. The event cost $225 per scan and $4,500 per qualified opportunity. Only the second number tells you whether to go back.

Freeman found that only **23%** of attendees came to identify people at provider companies to continue discussions with after the event. Most opportunities from an event take longer than the event. Track open relationships apart from active deals, and review the scorecard 30 and 90 days out. There is no reliable public benchmark yet for conference lead-to-opportunity conversion or cost per qualified meeting at Seed or Series A, so build your own from two or three events.

## Run the event in order

:::highlight
Ten steps from the decision to attend to the scorecard you review a quarter later.
:::

:::steps Conference to customers
1. **Write the outcome.** One line: what the event should produce and how you will know.
2. **Set the budget.** Money and founder days, follow-up hours included.
3. **Pick the format.** Attend, speak, host, or sponsor (Fitzpatrick).
4. **Build the list.** Speakers, sponsors, your network, and public posts.
5. **Prepare each contact.** Problem, known facts, guesses, and a reason to meet.
6. **Book meetings early.** A week or two out, with a time and a place.
7. **Staff for expertise.** Founders or engineers who know the buyer’s work (Freeman).
8. **End on a next step.** A date, an owner, and who else decides.
9. **Follow up within a day.** Their problem, your promise, the next step (Oldroyd, McElheran, and Elkington).
10. **Score the event.** Qualified opportunities and cost per opportunity at 30 and 90 days.
:::

## Mistakes that waste the trip

:::highlight
Each one fills a spreadsheet and leaves the pipeline where it was.
:::

**Buying a booth before writing the outcome.** Decide what you need first, then check whether the package delivers it.

**Arriving without meetings.** Book them before you fly. The hallway is for the people you missed.

**Staffing the booth with whoever is free.** Attendees want experts. Send the people who know the problem.

**Pitching the full product.** Show the part that solves their problem, then ask for the next step.

**Collecting scans without next steps.** A scan is permission to follow up under the event’s terms. It is not interest.

**Sending a generic blast a week later.** Write to each person about their conversation within a day.

**Judging the event on scan count.** Count qualified opportunities against the full cost.
