An internal champion is the person inside the buying company who has the standing to argue for your product in meetings you never attend, and a personal reason to do it. Find one by pain and power, then test them with a dated ask that costs them something, such as a meeting with the budget owner. Arm them with material written for the whole buying group, which Gartner puts at **5 to 16** people across up to four functions. Then reach the rest of that group yourself. In Gong's analysis of **1.8 million** deals closed in 2024, won deals had twice as many buyer contacts as lost deals, and engaging several contacts lifted win rates by **130%** on deals over **$50K**.

:::takeaways
- A champion needs power, a personal stake in your win, and proof they sell for you when you are absent. Someone with only one or two of those is a coach (MEDDICC).
- Test with a costly, dated ask: a meeting with the budget owner, the scoring sheet, a slot on the steering agenda. The action is the answer (Andy Whyte).
- Write for the group. In Gartner's 2025 survey of **632** buyers, content relevant to the whole group made a high-quality deal **3x** more likely; content tailored to one person cut consensus by **59%**.
- Thread the account. Winning deals had at least **3** buyer-side people in meetings, while losing deals rarely got past one (Gong, **52,000** opportunities).
- Track champions who change jobs. Deals with a past champion won at a **114%** higher rate in UserGems data, and **91%** of past champions did not come back on their own.
:::

This page covers one seat in the deal: the person who sells for you inside the account. If the champion is asking for a pilot, read [how to convert a pilot to a paid contract](/library/gtm/pilot-to-paid-contract/).

## Champion, coach, or contact

:::highlight
A friendly contact who takes your calls is a coach until they do something that costs them.
:::

MEDDICC, the qualification method many enterprise sales teams use, sorts the people you meet into three levels. Forrester's buying-group research defines the champion the same way from the buyer's side: the key advocate, with the credibility to persuade others to follow.

| Level | Power and influence | Personal stake in your win | Sells for you when you are absent | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Contact | Unknown | Unknown | Unknown | Learn their goals and who decides |
| Coach | Often missing | Maybe | Maybe | Use them for information and introductions |
| Champion | Yes | Yes | Yes, with evidence | Build the plan with them and test them again later |

Source: MEDDICC, champion definition and levels; Andy Whyte, the Champion Test.

Power is the one trait you cannot build. Andy Whyte, who wrote the book MEDDICC, calls it non-negotiable: it comes from how the company sees that person. You can grow the stake by tying your product to their own goals. They will not sell for you until they have that stake.

## Who else sits on the buying committee

:::highlight
The champion is one seat. Plan for the rest of the table from the first serious call.
:::

| Measure | Figure | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| People in a buying group | **5 to 16**, across up to **4** functions | Gartner, May 2025 (**632** buyers) |
| Buyer teams with unhealthy conflict during the decision | **74%** | Gartner, May 2025 |
| Groups that reach consensus, chance of a high-quality deal | **2.5x** more likely | Gartner, May 2025 |
| Internal stakeholders and outside influencers in a typical decision | **13** and **9** | Forrester, State of Business Buying 2026 |
| Contacts in large strategic deals that reps won | **17** on average | Gong Labs, 1.8M deals closed in 2024 |

Forrester names five roles in the group. The champion advocates. The decision-maker holds the final say and often the budget. Influencers shape the options, users live with the change, and ratifiers (often procurement or finance) handle cost and terms. Forrester notes that champions value information from the seller, and their rapport with sales, more than the other roles do. That makes the champion your best channel to the others, and a weak substitute for meeting them.

## Find the champion

:::highlight
Look for the person who owns the pain and has the standing to change how the team works.
:::

Start with the person whose number suffers if nothing changes. The VEN session view on targeting: the people who feel the pain are often missing from the first contact list, and they can push from the ground up faster than you can get a meeting with a senior executive ([VEN session](https://www.venexecs.com/?utm_source=founderdecisions&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=library&utm_content=internal-champion)). Check power before you invest: when this person speaks, do decisions change?

The warmest champion has bought from you before. UserGems tracked buyers and users who changed jobs and compared deals that included one of them with deals that did not.

| Deals that included a past champion or user | Result | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Win rate | **114%** higher | UserGems, **5,000+** opportunities |
| Deal size | **54%** larger | UserGems |
| Sales cycle | **12%** shorter | UserGems |
| Past champions at category leaders who came back unprompted after **90+** days in a new job | **9%** (so **91%** did not) | UserGems |

UserGems sells job-change tracking, and these figures come from its own analysis of customer data, so read them as direction. The practical step costs little: keep a list of the people who championed your first deals and contact them when they change jobs.

## Test the champion

:::highlight
Ask for something with a cost and a date. A coach goes quiet; a champion finds a way.
:::

Whyte's Champion Test checks the three traits with evidence, then makes an ask the person has to spend political capital on. Asking "are you our champion?" proves nothing, because everyone says yes.

| Ask | A champion | A coach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A meeting with the budget owner, with a date | Books it, or names who can and when | Explains the politics and books nothing |
| The evaluation scoring sheet or criteria | Sends it or walks you through it | Says it is confidential |
| The budget owner's reaction after your last meeting | Gives a straight account, bad news included | Says it went well |
| Your slot on the steering or approval agenda | Gets you on it | Promises to raise it |
| A dated plan to signature they co-edit | Edits it and adds the steps you missed | Leaves it unread |

Write down what they did. "Got us the budget owner meeting on the 14th" is evidence. "Very supportive" is not. Run the test again before the deal goes into your forecast and after any reorganization, because a champion who passed in month one can be a coach by month four (Whyte).

The VEN session version of the same test: make a co-edited, dated plan to purchase the price of a free trial, and put the meeting with the champion's boss and the other business units in it. If the champion will not set up those meetings, there is no trial.

## Arm the champion for the whole group

:::highlight
Give the champion the case the group needs to agree on, written so they can forward it.
:::

Gartner's 2025 survey is the strongest public evidence on what to hand over. Content tailored to the buying group improved consensus by **20%**. Content tailored to one person's priorities cut consensus by **59%**, because it hardens each member's own view. Buyers who saw content relevant to the whole group were **3x** more likely to report a high-quality deal.

| What the champion needs | Who it is for | Format |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One page with the problem, the outcome, price, and how to start | Budget owner | Price and terms on page one, plain numbers |
| The shared case: cost of the current process and the change for each function | The whole group | One document every member gets |
| Customer evidence from a similar company | Decision-maker | A reference call or a named case with numbers |
| Security packet and data handling | Security and IT | The document you send before they ask ([selling to the enterprise](/library/gtm/sell-to-enterprise/)) |
| Order form and standard terms | Procurement and legal | Your paper, ready early ([first customer contract](/library/gtm/first-customer-contract/)) |
| Answers to the objections they expect | The champion | A short list in their words |

The VEN session list is close to this one: a deck with the punchline first (price, what the buyer gets, how to trial on page one), a security document modeled on the public security pages of large software companies, customer evidence, and objection answers.

## Thread the account so a job change does not reset the deal

:::highlight
A single-threaded deal is at risk. Bring a new buyer-side person into each meeting.
:::

| Measure | Won deals | Lost deals | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Buyer-side people in meetings across the cycle | At least **3** | Rarely more than **1** | Gong Labs, **52,000** opportunities (2020) |
| Buyer contacts reached by email | **8** | **3** | Gong Labs (2020) |
| Buyer contacts per deal | About **2x** lost deals | Baseline | Gong Labs, **1.8M** deals closed in 2024 |
| Win-rate lift from multiple contacts, deals over **$50K** | **130%** | Baseline | Gong Labs (2025) |

Gong's data comes from its own customers' recorded calls and emails, so it skews toward companies that run a formal sales team. In its 2020 study, buyer-side attendance in the second meeting nearly tripled in deals that went on to win. In losing deals, the first contact was often the only person the seller ever talked to.

The champion should open those doors, and you should keep them open. VEN session judgment: score each stakeholder as positive, negative, or neutral, and give skeptical users one-on-one time during the pilot, because they often come around once they help shape it. If your champion leaves, the others keep the deal alive.

There is no reliable public benchmark yet for buying-group size or champion presence in Seed to Series A deals. The figures above come from large-company surveys and vendor datasets.

## Example: a champion who was a coach

Illustrative, not data from any deal. A Head of Operations loves your demo, forwards the deck, and says the VP will "definitely" approve. You ask for 30 minutes with the VP by the 14th. Two weeks pass with no meeting and a long explanation of a budget freeze. That is a coach. Keep them as a source, ask who owns the budget line, and find a second contact in finance or IT who feels the same pain. Leave the deal out of your forecast until someone with power books the meeting.

:::steps Find, test, and arm a champion
1. **List the pain owners.** Name the people whose number suffers if nothing changes, including users who are not on your first contact list.
2. **Check power.** Ask who decides and watch whose view changes the room. Power you cannot build.
3. **Find their stake.** Tie your product to the goal their boss judges them on.
4. **Run the test.** One costly, dated ask: the budget owner meeting, the scoring sheet, or the steering slot. Write down what happened.
5. **Write the plan together.** A dated path to signature, co-edited, with the boss and the other functions in it.
6. **Arm them for the group.** One shared case, a price-first page, customer evidence, the security packet, and your paper.
7. **Thread the account.** Add a new buyer-side person at each meeting and score each one positive, negative, or neutral.
8. **Re-test before forecast.** Run the test again before commit and after any reorganization.
9. **Track the champion.** If they change jobs, keep the deal threaded and contact them at the new company.
:::

## Mistakes founders make with champions

- **Champion equals deal.** One enthusiastic person carries the forecast. Gartner's **74%** conflict figure is the reason to meet the rest of the group.
- **Calling a coach a champion.** Whyte traces most "surprise" losses he inspected to this.
- **Cold-pitching the top.** VEN session judgment: a cold note to the CIO goes unanswered. Win the people who feel the pain and let the champion take you up.
- **Personalizing everything.** Messages tailored to one stakeholder cut group consensus by **59%** in Gartner's data.
- **Testing once.** People change roles. Test again before you forecast the deal.
