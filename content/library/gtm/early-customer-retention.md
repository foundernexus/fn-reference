Keep your first customers by running feedback as a loop you close. Write each request down as the task the customer tried to finish, what stopped them, and what it cost. Give it one owner and a date for the next update. Decide fix, plan, defer, or decline, and tell the customer which, including what waits and why. After a change ships, ask whether they can now finish the original task. Review continued use and cancellations in the same meeting as new sales, and slow intake when the team cannot serve the customers it already has. ChartMogul’s 2023 retention report found that top-quartile SaaS companies kept **90%** of new customers through the first **3** months and **70%** through the first **12**.

:::takeaways
- Log the blocked task. The request is the customer’s guess at a fix. The task is the fact you can test.
- Every item gets an owner, a decision, and an update date. A date for the next update is a different promise from a delivery date.
- Say no out loud. Des Traynor of Intercom calls “this customer is about to quit” feature blackmail. Explain the priority instead of building for one account.
- Close the loop on the task. Ask whether the customer can now do the work. A closed ticket records what your team did.
- Read retention beside new sales. ChartMogul ties low **3**-month retention to onboarding problems or bad-fit customers. There is no reliable public benchmark yet for logo retention across a startup’s first ten customers.
:::

## New sales can hide a retention problem

:::highlight
Put new customers, active use, and cancellations on one page. Signed contracts alone cannot tell you whether the product works.
:::

A team that wins logos while reliability problems stay open builds a backlog of support and product work. Revenue can flatten later when departures offset new sales. ChartMogul’s advice: before you assume a retention problem, check whether you have an acquisition problem, such as selling to customers who were a bad fit.

| Review weekly | What to look at | Act when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| New customers | Signed, by segment and source | A new segment arrives before the current one is served |
| Active use | Accounts doing the core task this week | A paying account goes quiet for two check-ins |
| Open blockers | Tasks customers cannot finish, by age | The oldest blocker has no owner or no update date |
| Onboarding load | Hours per new account in the first month | New accounts take hours the team does not have |
| Cancellations and downgrades | Who left, the reason in their words | Two departures share a reason |

Paul Graham, in Do Things That Don’t Scale: “How well you’re doing a few months later will depend more on how happy you made those users than how many there were of them.” He also wrote that he had “never once seen a startup lured down a blind alley by trying too hard to make their initial users happy.”

Lenny Rachitsky and Casey Winters asked **20** growth practitioners what good looks like. For SMB and mid-market SaaS, they put **6**-month retention of paying companies at about **60%** for good and about **80%** for great. Their note for early companies: startups rarely increase retention significantly, so use the bar to decide between retention work and acquisition work.

## Record the task behind the request

:::highlight
A feature request is the customer’s proposed fix. Write down the job they were trying to get done.
:::

The Christensen Institute describes Jobs to Be Done as the progress a person is trying to make in a particular circumstance. People “hire” a product to do that job. In its condominium case, focus groups asked for bay windows. Bob Moesta’s interviews with buyers found the move itself was the blocker, down to what to do with the dining room table. The builder changed the offer around the move and raised prices by **$3,500**.

| Request type | What it tends to mean | Default move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Defect | Something you promised does not work | Fix first. Tell each affected customer once it ships |
| Usability problem | The capability exists and the customer cannot find or finish it | Watch them try the task, then fix the path |
| Missing step in the core task | The job stops partway inside your product | Plan it if other customers in the segment hit the same wall |
| Request outside your market | A job your product was not built for | Decline and explain the line you drew |
| One large account’s demand | A fix for one customer that other customers would not use | Decline, or price it as separate work in a signed agreement |
| Request tied to a pilot or contract term | A blocker on something you agreed to deliver | Handle it against the written scope and success criteria |

Ask what the customer tried to do, what happened, and what it cost them. Keep their account apart from your reading of it. Get enough detail to reproduce the problem, and keep sensitive customer data in your approved support tool, out of a shared doc.

## Decide, then explain what will wait

:::highlight
A clear no with a reason keeps more trust than a maybe with no date.
:::

Traynor’s 2013 essay Product strategy means saying no lists the arguments that sneak features into a product. One of them is “But this customer is about to quit.” His reply: “No customer can be more important than a good product.” The road to consulting-ware starts with one exception for one customer. Graham draws the same line from the other side: once a customer pays you for attentiveness by the hour, they expect you to do everything.

Basecamp’s Getting Real says to start with no and let the requests that matter come back. Its team kept a tally list of feature requests and never looked at it again, because customers repeated the important ones. Keep that discipline and still log the task: the log tracks blocked work and promised updates, and the vote count does not set the roadmap.

The update to the customer, in order:

1. Restate the problem you understood. Ask them to correct it.
2. Show what changed and how to try it.
3. Name what remains open and the reason for the current priority.
4. Give the date of the next update, and say that it is not a delivery date.
5. Ask whether the open issue still blocks their work.

Hold back on promising a fix before someone has assessed the work. If a customer stops coming to check-ins after giving useful feedback, write to them about what changed in their work. An apology does not secure a renewal, and silence does not mean they are satisfied.

## Close the loop on the task

:::highlight
Done means the customer can do the work. A merged pull request is your side of it.
:::

Keep one log row per blocked task:

| Field | What to write |
| --- | --- |
| Customer and segment | Account reference and the job they bought you for |
| Blocked task and evidence | What they tried, what happened, when, and how you saw it |
| Impact | Who is affected, how often, and the workaround they use today |
| Commitment affected | Contract term, pilot success criterion, or none |
| Decision and owner | Fixed, planned, deferred, declined, or needs more information, and who decided |
| Next update | Who tells the customer, and the date |
| Confirmation | The customer’s answer to whether they can now finish the task |

Pendo’s 2019 Feature Adoption Report studied **615** subscriptions and found **80%** of features in the average product rarely or never used, with **12%** of features producing **80%** of daily usage. Measure use of what you ship for a customer. A fix nobody uses did not close the loop.

## Match acquisition to delivery capacity

:::highlight
Count the hours your current customers need before you add more of them.
:::

Before you push acquisition harder, add up the hours for onboarding, support, open fixes, and contract commitments. Name who will do that work if ten more accounts sign next month. If you cap intake, write down the constraint and the evidence that would lift the cap. A cap is a stage decision. Growth targets still apply once the team can serve what it sells.

Write the feedback terms into the deal. A design partner’s participation and feedback duties belong in [the first customer contract](/library/gtm/first-customer-contract/). In a pilot, handle issues against the agreed scope and success criteria from [how to convert a pilot to a paid contract](/library/gtm/pilot-to-paid-contract/). A new request mid-pilot should not replace the buying decision the pilot exists to settle.

## Read complaints and silence with care

:::highlight
Loud and quiet customers both need a question. Each signal answers a different one.
:::

An angry user cares about a blocked task. Their frustration does not prove product-market fit or a renewal. A quiet customer may be satisfied, busy, disengaged, or already on another tool. Ask, check their use, and confirm they still get the value you agreed to deliver.

ChartMogul’s report gives you the timing to watch. Its new-customer retention curve decays fastest in the first months, then drops again in months **11** and **12** as annual plans come up for renewal. Its tips include exit interviews after cancellation: ask whether price or a missing capability drove the decision.

| Signal | What it answers | What it does not answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A reply to your update | They still read you | Whether they use the product |
| A resolved issue | Your team acted | Whether the task now works for them |
| Weekly use of the core task | The product does a job for them | Whether they will pay again |
| A renewal | They paid again | Why, unless you ask |
| A referral | They will stake their name on you | How many peers share the problem |

## Measure early retention by cohort

:::highlight
Group customers by the month they signed. Read each group at **3** and **12** months.
:::

| Metric | Definition | Published reference |
| --- | --- | --- |
| New-customer logo retention | Customers still paying ÷ customers who signed in the cohort | ChartMogul top quartile: **90%** at **3** months, **70%** at **12** |
| Net revenue retention (NRR) | This month’s revenue from a cohort ÷ its revenue a year ago | ChartMogul top quartile at **$1–3M** ARR: **94%** |
| Gross revenue retention (GRR) | NRR without expansion, capped at **100%** | ChartMogul best-in-class: over **86%** at any stage |
| Paying-company retention at 6 months | Companies active **6** months after starting to pay | Rachitsky and Winters, SMB and mid-market: about **60%** good, **80%** great |
| Blocker age | Days a blocked task waits for a decision and an update | No reliable public benchmark yet |

ChartMogul found a wide spread in customer retention under **$1M** ARR, because only a few companies at that size have strong product-market fit. Treat the published figures as a bar for later stages. There is no reliable public benchmark yet for logo retention across a startup’s first ten customers or for time to close a feedback loop, so set your own bar from your cohorts.

## Run the feedback loop in order

:::highlight
Ten steps from the first signature to a retention review you can show a board.
:::

:::steps Early retention loop
1. **Open one log.** One row per blocked task, kept in your support tool.
2. **Write the task.** What the customer tried, what happened, and what it cost (Christensen Institute).
3. **Sort the request.** Defect, usability, missing step, outside the market, or one account’s demand.
4. **Assign an owner.** One person decides and one person updates the customer.
5. **Decide.** Fixed, planned, deferred, declined, or needs more information (Traynor; Getting Real).
6. **Send the update.** What changed, what waits, why, and the next update date.
7. **Confirm the task.** Ask whether they can now do the work, and check their use (Pendo).
8. **Review weekly.** New customers, active use, blockers, onboarding hours, and departures on one page.
9. **Set intake.** Add customers at the rate the team can onboard and support.
10. **Read the cohort.** Logo retention at **3** and **12** months, NRR, and GRR (ChartMogul).
:::

## Mistakes that lose the first customers

:::highlight
Each one feels like progress while the base drains.
:::

**Building the request as written.** Build for the task behind it.

**Promising a ship date to end a hard call.** Promise the next update date, then keep it.

**Closing the ticket and walking away.** Confirm the customer can finish the task.

**Building for the account that threatens to leave.** Traynor calls it feature blackmail. Explain the priority or price the work.

**Reading silence as satisfaction.** Ask what changed and check their use.

**Adding customers faster than you can onboard them.** Count support and onboarding hours first.

**Benchmarking ten customers against a scaled SaaS median.** Read your own cohorts. ChartMogul’s spread under **$1M** ARR is wide for a reason.
